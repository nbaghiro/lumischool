# The ukulele and the guitar

Status: proposed, September 2026. Built in the scratchpad: `src/sound/fretted.ts` for the instrument as data, `src/sound/fretguide.ts` for guided playing, `src/sound/pluck.ts` for the plucked string, `src/sound/neck.ts` for the playable neck, `src/art/strings.ts` for the drawings, `src/pages/music-strings.ts` for the page, five lessons in `content/lessons/`, and `test/strings.test.ts`. Play it at `music.html?i=uke` and `music.html?i=guitar`. Listening through the microphone is designed in this document and not built, because it is the owner's decision.

## Summary

A guitar is harder to show a child than a piano for one reason above the others: the three pictures a child meets of the same instrument disagree about where the strings are. A chord box stands the neck up with the lowest string on the left. Tab lays it down with the thinnest string on top. And when a child holds the instrument and looks down at it, the string nearest their chin is at the top of what they see, which is the reverse of tab. A keyboard has none of this, because it only ever faces one way.

We compared three ways of drawing a piece around one playable neck, built all three on the music page with a toggle between them, and recommend a combination. For a child of five to seven on a ukulele, the neck is drawn stood up facing them, the same way round as every chord card they will ever read, with a card for each chord or note and the strum drawn as arrows on the beat. From about eight, on a guitar, tab arrives, and it is introduced as that same card turned a quarter turn, which a child can do with the tablet in their hands. On screen the tab becomes a lane the notes travel along; printed, it is tab spaced by time. The view from above, as a child sees their own neck, is used for the one lesson about holding the instrument and for the drawing of the fretting hand, and its mirror is offered to the grown-up who teaches sitting opposite.

The child plays the neck on the screen, and a real instrument in the room is marked by the grown-up who hears it. We recommend keeping it that way. Web MIDI is the next input worth having if the owner wants one, and only for keyboards. Listening through the microphone can be done on the device, but it is fragile for a beginner's playing, it would break a promise we already show to parents, and the choice belongs to the owner.

## Why a neck is harder than a keyboard

The research behind this section is listed at the end, with what each source gave us.

A pitch on a guitar is a string and a fret, so the same pitch can be played in several places ([Wikipedia, Guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar)). The only landmarks are the fret markers at frets 3, 5, 7 and 9 and a double marker at 12, repeated as side dots "small enough to be visible only to the player" ([Fingerboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerboard)). There is nothing like the piano's groups of two and three black keys, which is what a child uses to find middle C before they know any letters. The tuning is not regular either: guitar strings are a fourth apart except between G and B, which are a major third ([Standard tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_tuning)), so a shape moved across the strings does not stay the same chord.

The numbering runs against a child's intuition. The thickest string is string 6 and the thinnest is string 1 ([Yousician, guitar string names](https://yousician.com/blog/guitar-string-names)). On a ukulele it is stranger: the G string nearest the player's face is tuned above the C beside it, so "the fourth string is actually higher than the third string" ([Wikipedia, Ukulele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukulele); [Yousician, tuning your ukulele](https://yousician.com/blog/tuning-your-ukulele)).

The two hands do different jobs. The left hand chooses a note by pressing a string, which makes no sound, and the right hand sounds it by plucking or strumming. The left hand's fingers are numbered 1 for the pointer finger to 4 for the little finger ([Classical guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_guitar)), which is not the pianist's numbering, where the thumb is 1. The right hand has a rhythm of its own: a strum has a direction, and the rule every teacher gives first is that the hand keeps moving down and up whether it touches the strings or not ([Wikibooks, Guitar/Rhythm](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Guitar/Rhythm)).

### The orientation problem

The chord box and tab are the two written forms a child will meet, and they are drawn at right angles to each other. In a chord box "the vertical lines represent the six guitar strings", with the low E on the left and the nut as the thick line at the top ([Yousician, chord charts](https://yousician.com/blog/guitar-chord-charts)). In tab the strings are horizontal lines with the high E on top and the low E at the bottom ([Yousician, reading tabs](https://yousician.com/blog/how-to-read-guitar-tabs)). Teachers explain the chord box as the instrument "positioned in a vertical, upright position" ([Ukulele Tricks, chords](https://ukuleletricks.com/ukulele-chords/)) and tab as the strings upside down: "the top line represents the bottom string of the ukulele" ([Ukulele Tricks, tabs](https://ukuleletricks.com/how-to-read-ukulele-tabs/)). Neither convention is universal. Some tab is written the other way up ([Wikipedia, Tablature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablature)), and one vendor's own ukulele article draws chord diagrams on their side with G at the bottom ([Yousician, ukulele chords](https://yousician.com/blog/ukulele-chords)).

Drawn out, there are four pictures of the same neck, and they are related by turning and by mirroring. We named them in the code and use the names throughout:

| View | How it is drawn | Where the string nearest the chin is | Related to the chart by |
|---|---|---|---|
| chart | stood up facing you, nut at the top | on the left | itself |
| tab | on its side, nut on the left, string 1 on top | at the bottom | a quarter turn |
| player | on its side, nut on the left, string 1 at the bottom | at the top | a quarter turn and a flip |
| mirror | the player's view with the nut on the right | at the top | a quarter turn, a flip and a flip |

The fact the whole design rests on is in the last column. The chart and tab are a rotation apart, so a child can turn a tablet showing a chord card through a quarter turn and watch it become tab. The view from the child's own eyes is a mirror image of tab, and a mirror image has to be imagined rather than watched. We think a rotation is easier to explain to a child of seven than a mirror image, since it can be shown, and we have not tested this with children. Chord boxes come before tab in the course we looked at most closely, JustinGuitar, which teaches "How To Read Guitar Chord Boxes" before the first chord and tab four modules later ([JustinGuitar, Module 0](https://community.justinguitar.com/c/beginner-guitar-course-grade-one/before-you-begin/177); [Module 4](https://community.justinguitar.com/c/beginner-guitar-course-grade-one/using-a-metronome-riffs-and-tab/173)).

## How children are taught

### The ukulele first

In schools the ukulele is increasingly the first fretted instrument, "sometimes replacing the recorder as first musical instrument" ([Wikipedia, Ukulele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukulele)). The Canadian school programme J. Chalmers Doane began in the 1960s had about 50,000 learners at its peak, and he and James Hill created Ukulele in the Classroom in 2008 ([Doane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Chalmers_Doane)), a "sequential, performance-based" method covering singing, picking, strumming, ear training, sight reading and improvising ([Ukulele in the Classroom](https://www.ukuleleintheclassroom.org/our-story)). A soprano ukulele has a scale of about 13 to 13.6 inches and nylon strings, and the open strings G, C, E and A make a chord on the first day, which is why a child can strum before they can fret.

We found no single authoritative source for the common pattern of ukulele at five to seven and guitar from eight to ten. The nearest is Loog's own age steps for its small guitars, 3 and up, 6 and up and 9 and up ([Loog FAQ](https://loogguitars.com/pages/faqs)). We follow the pattern because the sources that do speak to it agree in direction, and say plainly that the ages are ours.

Children's guitars come in quarter, half and three-quarter sizes and smaller, with scale lengths of about 484 to 578 mm against a full-size classical guitar's 650 mm ([Classical guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_guitar); [Scale length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_length_(string_instruments))). They are nearly always nylon-strung, and nylon is kinder to a new finger: learners report the soreness gone in about two weeks ([JustinGuitar forum](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/how-to-minimize-finger-pain/1766)) to a month ([Yousician, calluses](https://yousician.com/blog/guitar-calluses)).

### Two traditions

One tradition reads first. Hal Leonard's Guitar Method teaches "notes in first position" before chords, and its Guitar for Kids uses "no tablature" ([Hal Leonard 699010](https://www.halleonard.com/product/699010/hal-leonard-guitar-method-book-1); [Guitar for Kids](https://www.halleonard.com/product/viewproduct.action?itemid=865003&name=guitar-for-kids)). Suzuki starts at three to five, learns by ear before reading, and puts a parent at every lesson ([Suzuki method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzuki_method)). Ukulele in the Classroom starts with notes on the open strings and a handout to "show students how to play notes without TAB", and its starter kit warns against "the C Chord Trap" ([teaching tools](https://www.ukuleleintheclassroom.org/teaching-tools); [starter kit](http://www.ukuleleintheclassroom.org/starterkit)).

The other plays chords first. JustinGuitar's first grade teaches two chords in its first module, then strumming on the beat, then up strums and counting "ands", then tab and a first strumming pattern, and it drills changes with One Minute Changes: a minute of switching between two chords, counted and written down, where first attempts on the forum range from 17 to 65 changes ([Grade 1 modules](https://community.justinguitar.com/c/beginner-guitar-course-grade-one/34); [One Minute Changes](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/one-minute-changes-exercise/2367)). Fender Play, Simply Guitar and Yousician are chord-first and song-first as well.

We take from both. The first ukulele lessons name the open strings and play them one at a time, which is the reading tradition's start, and then read a chord card for one finger, which is the chord tradition's. First chords differ between sources: for the ukulele C, Am, F and G7 appear early in every list we read, and for the guitar the lists start from Em or E ([Yousician, easy chords](https://yousician.com/blog/9-easy-chords-for-beginners); [Hal Leonard Ukulele for Kids](https://www.halleonard.com/product/696468/ukulele-for-kids-the-hal-leonard-ukulele-method)). For small hands, JustinGuitar teaches a two-finger G ([forum](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/the-g-chord-hacked/2394)) and Loog's three strings "reduce chords to triads" ([Loog Mini](https://loogguitars.com/products/loog-mini-kids-guitar)). Our guitar book has three-string shapes for G, C, G7 and Em for the same reason; the one-finger shapes are common teaching practice and we did not find a source that documents them.

Strumming is written as letters, "d du udu", or as arrows ([Strum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strum)), with the down strum on the numbers and the up strum on the "and". Cynthia Lin's free ukulele series teaches the Island Strum as its fourth lesson ([lessons](https://ukulele101withcynthialin.weebly.com/beginner-lessons.html)). Tuning is done with a clip-on tuner, which reads the vibration through the head and so ignores room noise ([Electronic tuner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_tuner)), and new strings keep drifting for their first week ([Wikibooks, tuning](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Guitar/Tuning_the_Guitar)). No source we read says a parent should tune for a young child; we recommend it in the lessons' notes for grown-ups because a child of six cannot hear or fix a string that is a little flat.

### How the apps show it

We looked at how the good apps and courses draw a neck, to take ideas and not visuals.

Yousician draws tab-like horizontal strings with coloured numbers approaching from the right, and on the ukulele the colour says which finger to use ([Yousician, reading tabs](https://yousician.com/blog/how-to-read-guitar-tabs); [ukulele](https://yousician.com/ukulele)). Its new children's app, WildChords, released in July 2026 for ages four and up, gives each animal "its own chord", C for the crocodile, and listens through the microphone; one early review says the first song "won't pick up and when it does it's way late" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/yousician-guitar-wildchords/id6768210195)). Rocksmith sends notes from the background towards the player along lanes that are frets, with colour meaning the string ([Rocksmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocksmith)). Guitar Hero shows the neck vertically with gems falling towards the player ([Guitar Hero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar_Hero)), and Rock Band 3's pro mode puts fret numbers on the gems, which it says closely resembles tab ([Rock Band 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_Band_3)). Fender Play pairs short videos with scrolling tab and chord charts ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/fender-play-learn-guitar/id1226057939)); JustinGuitar's app shows the next chord's diagram or the strumming pattern beside the lyrics ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/justin-guitar-lessons-songs/id1176125504)). The products made for young children change the hardware more than the notation: Loog removes three strings, Kala's Color Chord ukulele puts coloured stickers on the frets, one colour to a chord ([Kala](https://kalabrand.com/collections/learn-to-play-ukulele/products/kala-r-ltp-cc-s)), and ChordBuddy clamps coloured buttons onto the neck for two months ([ChordBuddy](https://www.chordbuddy.com/products/chordbuddy-guitar-learning-system/)).

Five things we took. Pick one orientation for each stage of learning and explain it once, with a picture that links it to the instrument in the child's hands. Teach the chord card before tab. Let colour mean one thing, and since the finger is the choice a child has to make, colour means the finger and never the string; Rocksmith and Yousician put colour on different things, and mixing both on one screen would overload it. Wait for the child rather than scroll past them unless they ask for time, which is the decision `src/sound/guide.ts` already made for the keyboard. And do not rest a beginner's marking on the microphone, which the apps that use it hedge with instructions to tune every time, find a quiet room and take the phone out of its case ([Simply help](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/5135082-improve-note-recognition-in-simply-guitar)).

Colour is also where the apps are weakest on access. Of those we checked, only the JustinGuitar app declares "Differentiate Without Color Alone", and Rocksmith 2014 had a colour-blind mode ([Rocksmith 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocksmith_2014)). Up to 8 per cent of men of Northern European ancestry have a colour vision deficiency ([Color blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_blindness)). Our finger dots carry the number as well as the colour, and on paper each of the four colours becomes a different hatch with a white patch under the number, so nothing depends on colour; that is the ink rule doing what it does everywhere else on the shelf.

## Three directions

All three are built on the music page around the same playable neck, with the same pieces, so they can be compared on one tune. The toggle is a row of three buttons at the top of the strings view, `[` and `]` step through them the way they step through the parent pages, and the choice is kept in the URL: `music.html?i=uke&v=cards`, `v=neck` or `v=lane`. Under the stage the page draws the same piece for paper in each direction side by side.

### A. Cards

The neck is stood up facing the child, the chart view, with the head and its pegs at the top and the body below the frets, where the strings are strummed across the sound hole. Beside it runs a row of cards: for a strummed piece one chord card for each bar, the one being played ringed and captioned "now"; for a melody one card for each note, a chord box with a single dot in it, named by its letter. Under the cards the strum track draws the bar's pattern as arrows, with the arrow being played ringed. When a chord changes, the chord change card shows only the fingers that move: a heavy ring for the finger that stays, a dashed ring and an arrow for one that moves, and a dashed ring for one that lifts.

It prints as a chord card and a strum track, both small, in whole squares: a ukulele card is 10 by 16 squares, so three fit across a page with the strum track under them. That is how chord charts are printed everywhere, so a family's own songbook reads the same way.

At five a child reads the card as a picture to copy: a coloured dot with a number on it, on a line, and the neck beside it the same way round. Holding the ukulele up next to the screen shows the strings matching, which is the explanation teachers give. At nine the same cards read as a chord chart, and the strum track as a pattern to keep.

From one string to full chords it scales without changing shape. A single note is a card with one dot, the open strings are a card with no dots, one-finger C is a card with one dot, and a four-fingered chord is the same card with four. The neck is always the same way round as the card.

### B. My neck

The neck is drawn as the child sees their own when they look down at it, the player view, nut on the left and the string nearest the chin at the top, with the fretting hand drawn under it from above: four fingers numbered 1 to 4 and coloured the way the dots are, and the thumb marked T. The place to play is ringed on the neck with its finger number, the same finger is ringed on the hand, and a line in big writing says it: "String 3, fret 2, finger 2". A second toggle turns the neck round to the mirror view, as a teacher sitting opposite holds theirs, which is how a child sees a grown-up demonstrate.

It prints as a neck with the dots on it and the hand under it. That is the least compact of the three, and nothing a family already has is drawn this way.

At five it is the most direct: the picture is the child's own view, and the hand says which finger. At nine it becomes a problem, because every chord chart and every tab the child meets is drawn another way round, one of them as a mirror image of this.

From one string to full chords it scales well on screen, since a chord is simply several dots on the neck, and poorly in print, where a neck is three times the size of a chord card for the same information.

### C. Lane

Tab turned into a lane. The neck is laid down in the tab view with string 1 on top, and the lane starts where the body ends: a band for each string at the neck's own spacing, so each string runs on out of the instrument into its lane, a heavy line just past the body where a note is played, and each note a stone with its fret written on it, placed along its string by when it comes. On a phone there is not room for the two side by side, and the lane goes above the neck. In the child's own time the lane slides one note along each time the right place is played; in time, it slides at the tempo and the timing is judged at the end, with the window drawn the way the keyboard's beat track draws it. Stones that have been played go under the line.

It prints as tab spaced by time, four squares to a beat, with the beats ruled across, which says the one thing tab cannot: how long each note lasts ([Wikibooks, Tablature](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Guitar/Tablature)).

At five the moving stones are the most game-like of the three, but the reading is tab, and tab is the orientation that is furthest from what the child sees. At nine it is a sensible way to read a single-note line and to play along, and the printed form is a real tab.

From one string to full chords it scales awkwardly: a chord is a column of stones joined by a bar, which is legible for three strings and crowded for six, and a strum has a direction a stone cannot show.

### Compared

| | A. Cards | B. My neck | C. Lane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orientation | the chord card's own | the child's own eyes | tab's |
| Matches printed music the family already has | yes, chord charts | no | yes, tab |
| How it prints | small cards and a strum track | a neck and a hand, three times the size | tab spaced by time |

| Motion | none | none | slides, and stops under reduced motion |

## The recommendation

Cards for the ukulele, from five to seven, with the strum track under them and the chord change card for every change. It is the only direction that matches the printed music a family already has, it prints compactly, it is the same card from one note to four fingers, and it is the one that shows a strum's direction as something drawn rather than something said. It is also the one that fits a phone held upright, which is how a child holding an instrument will prop one up.

The lane and printed tab from about eight, on the guitar, introduced in a lesson as the chord card turned a quarter turn. The page can show the turn, and the drawings are the same neck geometry, `neckShape` in `src/art/strings.ts`, so the rotation is exact. The lane is the right way to show time on screen, and its printed form is a real tab with the durations it usually lacks.

The view from above only where it earns its place: the lesson about holding the instrument, the drawing of the fretting hand (`hand` on the shelf), and the mirror view for a grown-up teaching face to face. We would not make it the reading view, because the child would then have to unlearn it twice.

## How the instrument is drawn

The first version drew the neck as a chord box turned four ways: a heavy nut, the strings as lines, thin fret lines on the squared paper and the sound hole as a pencil circle. It was correct and it did not look like an instrument. The frets were the weight of the paper's grid and disappeared into it, nothing showed which end was the head, and a circle with nothing round it read as a circle. A child sees the picture before reading anything on it, so the picture has to look like the instrument they are holding.

The neck is now drawn as the instrument close up, the way the keyboard is drawn as keys. There is a headstock with its pegs, three a side on the guitar and two on the ukulele, and the posts the strings wind on; a bone nut; a dark fingerboard with silver frets across it and pearl dots at the third, fifth, seventh and ninth frets; the strings at their thicknesses, with the guitar's three low strings wound and darker; and past the last fret the body, its shoulders curving out from the heel of the neck, some grain on the top and the sound hole with its rosette. The body is the strum zone, so a child strums where a real hand does.

The instrument is drawn once, lying down with the head on the left, and each view is that drawing turned: stood up for the chart, as it is for tab, flipped for the child's own view and mirrored for a teacher opposite. `neckShape` in `src/art/strings.ts` carries the transform with the geometry, so the targets and the drawing cannot disagree. Only the instrument itself is turned. The places, the finger dots, the rings and every word are drawn in the view, so no number ends up on its side.

The palette has no brown, so the woods are mixed from it: the guitar's spruce top from the yellow and the orange, the ukulele's koa from the orange and the ink, the rosewood fingerboard darker again, bone for the nut and silver for the frets. Everything that has to be read on the dark board has a pale rim: the strings, the finger dots, the ring that says "play this one" and the keyboard's dashed ring. On paper each wood is a hatch of its own (dots for the top, a shallow hatch for the fingerboard and a steep one for the head), and the frets and the nut stay white so they still cut across it.

The neck is wider than life. A place has to clear 44 pixels, so every string is two squares from the next and every fret is three squares long all the way up, where a real fretboard is about seven times longer than it is wide and its frets close up towards the body. The head and the sound hole are drawn to the drawn neck rather than to a real one. When the body is long enough the whole sound hole shows, and when it is short a larger one runs off the end of the box, the way a photograph of a neck shows it.

The whole instruments on the shelf, `guitar` and `ukulele`, keep the real proportions, since nothing on them is played. Their frets are placed by the rule a luthier uses, each one closer to the last by the twelfth root of two, and the neck meets the body at the twelfth fret, halfway to the saddle. The guitar has a spruce top, a pickguard and a bridge with its saddle and pins; the ukulele has a koa top and a tie bridge. Both can name their parts with a line to each (tuning pegs, head, nut, frets, neck, strings, sound hole, saddle, bridge and body), and both can ring one string.

## The playable neck

The neck is the `fretboard` part, the same declaration as the keyboard with the keys renamed, and `src/sound/neck.ts` mounts it. It has one real difference from the keyboard's mount: a finger stays where it is put.

A tap on a place puts a finger there and plucks that string, so a melody is one tap a note, the way the keyboard is. The finger stays down until it is moved to another fret on the same string or lifted by tapping the open column above the nut, which also plucks the open string, the way lifting a finger does on a real instrument. A sweep across the strum zone sounds every string it crosses with whatever is held on it, in the order it met them; a sweep towards string 1 is a down strum whichever way the neck is drawn, because down is where the hand goes and not a direction on the screen. One string touched in the strum zone is a pluck. For a child who cannot sweep, and for the keyboard, there are Strum down and Strum up buttons, and a Lift fingers button.

The computer keyboard reaches everything. The neck is one tab stop; the arrow keys move a dashed ring from place to place by what is next to it on the screen, so the arrows mean the same thing in every view; Space or Enter plays the ringed place; the number keys pluck a string by its number; D strums down and U strums up. The dashed ring shows only for keyboard movement, since a finger on the glass already knows where it is. Every place is a button to a screen reader, named "E4, string 2, open", with `aria-pressed` while a finger is on it.

One string sounds one note. Every note goes on a channel named after its string, and a new note on a string stops the one it was ringing, which is the one addition this work made to the `Sounder` interface in `src/sound/types.ts`. A strum crosses the strings over about 14 milliseconds a string going down and 10 coming up, an up strum is lighter, and the strings a chord leaves out are drawn with a cross and are crossed without sounding, which is what a teacher's "strum only the thin strings" becomes on a screen.

Every place is two squares across, and a place has to clear the 44 pixel floor, so the page works out the square from the width it has rather than drawing at a fixed size. Measured in headless Chrome at 400 pixels wide, the smallest place is 44 pixels on the guitar in every direction and on the ukulele on its side, and 60 on the ukulele stood up, and the page does not scroll sideways. What gives way first is the body: on a phone a neck on its side has three frets, its string names and one square of the body, with the strum zone dropped and the strum buttons kept, and the string names go only if even that square does not fit.

A left-handed mode is not built. The mirror view is the picture a left-handed child sees of their own neck, so the drawing is ready; what is missing is the mount's setting and the lessons' wording. Yousician mirrors both the hand and the charts for left-handed players and Simply Guitar does not mirror at all ([Yousician](https://yousician.com/blog/left-handed-guitar); [Simply](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/5811497-i-m-left-handed-how-do-i-follow-the-lessons)).

## Guided playing

`src/sound/fretguide.ts` is the keyboard's guide with two differences. A step is a place and not only a pitch, so the child is told "string 3, fret 2, middle finger" rather than "D". And a step can be a strum, which sounds several strings at once, has a direction, and needs a shape held first.

What stays the same is the rule the keyboard's guide keeps: every verdict on pitch comes from `judgePitches` in `src/sound/judge.ts`, so there is one account of what counts as right, and a wrong note does not advance and makes no noise about itself. A pluck is right when the pitch is right, wherever it was played: E on the second string open and E on the third string at the fourth fret are the same answer, and the test in `test/strings.test.ts` plays exactly that. A strum is right when every string the shape plays sounds its note and the stroke goes the way the arrow does. When a strum is wrong the child is told the one thing to fix, in the words a teacher uses: "String 1 needs the ring finger on fret 3", "That was an up strum. This one goes down", "String 1 is played open here, so lift the finger off it".

"Hold them for me" puts the fingers on for the child, so a five-year-old can strum along before they can fret, which is how a teacher starts a class of young children; "I put them on" lights the places and waits. The performance records where each note was played as well as its pitch, `place: "s3f4"`, because a pitch alone would lose what a teacher would want to see.

## Hearing the child

There are four ways a child's playing can reach the product. This section sets out what each would take, how accurate it can be for a child on a small nylon-strung instrument, and what it means for the rule that no microphone or camera is captured. [sound.md](sound.md) records the microphone as a no, `src/family/privacy.ts` lists audio among what we do not collect and shows that list to parents, and `scripts/check-privacy.mjs` fails the build on any capture. This document does not change any of that. It prices the options so the owner can decide.

### On-screen playing

Built. The child taps and sweeps the neck drawn on the page, and the page knows the exact place and the exact moment of every note. Accuracy is total, because nothing is inferred. Its limits are that a screen is not an instrument: there is no fretting pressure, no buzz, and no way to tell a child who plays the screen well from one who plays a ukulele well. The privacy rule is untouched.

### A real instrument, with the page showing and counting

Built, as the third tier [sound.md](sound.md) describes: the page shows the card, plays the piece, counts the child in and keeps the beat, and the grown-up in the room hears the playing and is the marker. The lessons say so in their notes for grown-ups. Accuracy is whatever the grown-up's ear is, which for "did the C ring clearly" is better than any detector. The privacy rule is untouched.

### Web MIDI

Not built. A MIDI instrument sends note events, not sound: which note, how hard, and when. Web MIDI works in Chrome, Edge and Opera, in desktop Firefox from version 108 through a site permission add-on, and in Chrome on Android; it does not work in Safari on the Mac, iPhone or iPad, and WebKit lists it among the features it will not ship for now because of fingerprinting ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/midi); [WebKit](https://webkit.org/tracking-prevention/); [Firefox 108](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Mozilla/Firefox/Releases/108)). Since Chrome 124 the whole API sits behind a permission prompt, not only system exclusive messages ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/web-midi-permission-prompt)), and each port exposes its name and manufacturer, which the specification treats as a fingerprinting vector like a gamepad's ([Web MIDI](https://www.w3.org/TR/webmidi/)).

For a keyboard this is cheap and exact: a USB keyboard is an ordinary thing for a family to own, and Simply Piano already takes MIDI from one ([Simply help](https://piano-help.hellosimply.com/en/articles/3987010-bluetooth-headphones-and-bluetooth-midi)). For a guitar it is not. The cheapest USB MIDI guitar we found is the Jamstik Core at $649.99, with a three-quarter body but a full 25.5 inch scale ([Jamstik](https://jamstik.com/products/jamstik-core-midi-guitar)); the add-on pickups (Fishman TriplePlay, the BOSS GK-5) are for steel-string guitars ([Fishman](https://www.fishman.com/product/tripleplay-express/); [BOSS](https://www.boss.info/global/products/gk-5/)). None is child-scale and none suits a nylon-strung child's guitar. Tracking is claimed at under 10 milliseconds for the Jamstik and is a hexaphonic pickup's per-string signal, which would even give a strum's direction; nobody publishes accuracy for a child's fingers.

For privacy, a note stream is not audio and carries no voice. What would have to hold: request access without system exclusive messages, keep device names on the device and out of any event, and accept that a parent sees a browser prompt. It would not break the microphone rule.

### Listening through the microphone, on the device

Not built, and not ours to add. It can be done in the browser with nothing leaving the device. Monophonic pitch is the easy part: the McLeod pitch method is stable down to a window of two periods ([Tartini paper](https://www.cs.otago.ac.nz/graphics/Geoff/tartini/papers/A_Smarter_Way_to_Find_Pitch.pdf)), which for the guitar's low E at 82.41 Hz is 24 ms and in practice means a 2048-sample window of about 43 ms, and libraries under permissive licences exist (pitchy, 0BSD; CREPE's model, MIT, which ml5 runs in the browser with weights that can be self-hosted; Spotify's Basic Pitch, Apache-2.0, polyphonic) ([pitchy](https://github.com/ianprime0509/pitchy); [CREPE](https://github.com/marl/crepe); [ml5](https://github.com/ml5js/ml5-library/blob/main/docs/reference/pitch-detection.md); [basic-pitch-ts](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch-ts)). essentia.js is AGPL and aubio's builds carry the GPL, which rules them out for us ([essentia.js](https://mtg.github.io/essentia.js/); [aubio](https://aubio.org/manual/latest/cli.html)).

For a child's instrument the accuracy is the problem. CREPE's published accuracy is on re-synthesised audio ([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.06182)). Chord recognition was benchmarked on GuitarSet, six players each with more than ten years of experience, recorded in a studio; a baseline recogniser's triad recall was 0.84 on the chords the players were told to play and 0.71 on what they actually played, and multi-pitch accuracy on the microphone recording was about 43 per cent ([GuitarSet](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2018/paper/000188.pdf)). Onset detection that runs as the sound arrives reaches about 80 per cent ([Böck and others, 2012](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2012/paper/000049.pdf)). We found no study of children or beginners, no figures for a noisy room, and no evaluation of a strum's direction from one microphone. A beginner's first fretted notes buzz and are genuinely ambiguous, and a lesson that says the wrong note was played when the right one was played badly is worse than no lesson; the product's own browsers would also add echo cancellation and noise suppression unless we turned them off ([getUserMedia](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/MediaDevices/getUserMedia)).

For privacy, three things. Under the amended COPPA rule an audio file containing a child's voice is personal information and voiceprints are biometric identifiers ([16 CFR 312.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2)); the FTC's 2017 statement says an operator collects audio "as soon as the operator gathers the audio file ... regardless of how long" it is kept, and its exception is for audio used "solely as a replacement for written words" ([policy statement](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/1266473/coppa_policy_statement_audiorecordings.pdf)), which grading a guitar is not. The FTC's FAQ says an app is not collecting personal information "simply because your app interacts with personal information that is stored on the device and is never transmitted" ([FAQ F.5](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)), which is the nearest thing to cover for listening on the device, and it is about stored photos, not a microphone opened by our code. In Europe the EDPB says microphone use strictly inside the device is not access "as long as the information does not leave the device", but once "any derivation of this information" is accessed the ePrivacy Directive applies ([Guidelines 2/2023](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-22023-technical-scope-art-53-eprivacy-directive_en)), so a score worked out from the audio and sent to our server would engage it. And the browser would ask the parent for the microphone and show that it is live, which on an iPhone is an orange dot ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/HT211876)); a product that shows parents a list saying it never uses the microphone cannot then ask for it.

What it would take if the owner decided for it: a parent's switch that is off by default and says what it does in plain words; capture with the browser's echo cancellation and noise suppression off; pitch worked out in an AudioWorklet and the samples dropped as they are read; nothing derived from the audio leaving the device, not even a score; single notes only, for tuning and for a first melody, and never chords or strums; the privacy list rewritten, the guard given a reviewed exception for one file, and a lawyer's reading of FAQ F.5 against our case.

### What we recommend

This is the owner's decision. We recommend keeping the two that are built, the screen and the grown-up's ear, as the whole of it for now. If an input is wanted next, Web MIDI for keyboards, offered to the parent as an option, is cheap, exact and does not touch the microphone rule; for the guitar it has nothing a family could buy. We recommend not building listening, and if it is built, only on the terms above.

## The sound

A plucked string is a recursion: a burst of noise circulating round a loop one period long, losing a little of its top on every trip. That is Karplus and Strong's 1983 algorithm ([paper](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~karplus/papers/digitar.pdf)), and `src/sound/pluck.ts` adds the extensions Jaffe and Smith described the same year ([Stanford CCRMA](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Extended_Karplus_Strong_Algorithm.html)): a first-order allpass for the fraction of a sample the loop needs to be in tune; a three-tap loop filter with linear phase, so how dark the string is can change without moving its pitch; a comb on the burst at the point where the string is plucked, which removes the harmonics that have a node there; a soft lowpass on the burst for a finger rather than a pick; and two or three resonances for the body. The guitar's air resonance sits "somewhere between F#2 and A2" with another about an octave above ([UNSW](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/music/guitar/guitarintro.html)), so the nylon voice has peaks near 100, 205 and 410 Hz and the smaller ukulele near 275 and 480 Hz. The two voices, `nylon` and `uke`, are data a person can argue about, the way the piano's are in `strike.ts`, and nobody has listened to them against a real instrument in a room.

The piano is described to Web Audio as oscillators; a plucked string cannot be. A loop of DelayNodes is the obvious way to build one in Web Audio, and it does not work: a DelayNode in a cycle is clamped to at least one render quantum of 128 samples ([Web Audio source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/main/index.bs)), so no note above 344.5 Hz at 44.1 kHz, or 375 Hz at 48 kHz, can be played, and the ukulele's open G and A are both above it. Tone.js gets round this with an AudioWorklet ([Tone.js](https://github.com/Tonejs/Tone.js/blob/dev/Tone/component/filter/FeedbackCombFilter.ts)). We render each note to samples instead, the first time it is asked for, and keep it, which is what an older JavaScript guitar does too ([amid.fish](https://amid.fish/javascript-karplus-strong)). The reason is the one the piano was built on: the same function renders the note for the test and for the browser, so every claim about the sound is a measurement in a plain node run. An AudioWorklet would be a second module loaded at run time and could not be measured in a test.

Measured in `test/strings.test.ts` and in the numbers behind this document:

- Every note of both voices is within a quarter of a cent of its pitch at 44.1 and 48 kHz.
- A note's first tenth of a second is at 0.143 before the master gain, against the piano's 0.164, so the strings are about 1.2 dB quieter than a piano note and a strum of four or six is louder than one piano note, as it should be. The level is set from that first tenth of a second rather than from the peak, because the peak of a noise burst is luck: across one voice the peak over the level ran from three to nearly nine.
- A single note peaks between 0.40 and 0.62, a soft limit keeps a spike under the voice's ceiling, and a hard strum of all six strings of an E chord peaks at 0.36 after the master gain, well clear of full scale.
- The fifth harmonic of a G3 falls to under two thirds of its share of the note in its first second, which is the note turning dull as a string does.
- One guitar note takes about 3 ms to render at 48 kHz once the code is warm. The first strum of a new chord pays that for each string, about 15 ms for six, and the page asks for a chord's notes to be rendered as soon as the chord is shown, inside a context a gesture has already made, so the child's first strum is usually already paid for. Three renderings of each note are kept, with different noise, so a string plucked twice is not a copy of itself.

A handful of samples we recorded ourselves is the alternative, and we priced it. At 64 kbps mono Opus a three-second note is about 24 KB, so six strings with three samples each is about 430 KB, and iOS Safari has full Opus support only from 18.4 ([Opus settings](https://wiki.xiph.org/Opus_Recommended_Settings); [caniuse](https://caniuse.com/opus)). Pitching one sample to neighbouring frets with `playbackRate` shortens its decay and moves its body resonances with it ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioBufferSourceNode/playbackRate)). It is affordable, and it would add a recorded asset kind to a catalogue whose rule is that there is no generated or recorded sound beyond the voices declared in `src/sound/`; [sound.md](sound.md) already prices that fourth asset kind. We would only do it if a listening test in a room found the synthesised voices wanting.

The strings sit beside the piano without changing it: the same `Sounder`, the same master gain and eight-voice cap, and the quietest voice dropped when a ninth arrives, worked out for a plucked note from its declared decay. The old wave voice called `string` is gone, because the plucked voices replace it.

## Scope and sequence, ages five to ten

Our grades start at one, where a child is six or seven; a five-year-old can do the grade one ukulele lessons with a grown-up beside them. The ages in this table are ours, as said above.

| Grade and age | Instrument | What is learned |
|---|---|---|
| 1, six to seven (and five with help) | soprano ukulele | holding it; the parts; the four open strings G, C, E, A and their numbers; the guitarist's finger numbers; reading a chord card; C and A minor, one finger each; four down strums on the beat; one-chord songs; open-string melodies from cards |
| 2, seven to eight | soprano ukulele | F and G7; changes with the finger that stays, and a minute of changes counted each day; down and up strums and the Island Strum; two and three chord songs; melodies on the C and E strings from cards |
| 3, eight to nine | half or three-quarter nylon guitar | six strings and their numbers; tab, as the card turned a quarter turn; three-string chords for G, C and G7 and two-finger E minor; single-note lines on strings 1 to 3 from tab and the lane; play-along in time |
| 4, nine to ten | the same guitar | open chords C, G, D, A minor, E minor, E and A, and changes between them; strum patterns in four and in three; the first position notes on strings 1 to 3 read from the staff as well as tab; accompanying a song |

The notation can place all of them. `fretboard` has a hand-written registry entry, because its anchors are its places and depend on the tuning, and it is an input, so an item that uses it is answered by playing it. A new code checker, `music.chord chord=C tuning=uke`, proves the chord is in the book for that instrument, that a hand can make it (no finger on two strings, no more than four fingers, no stretch past four frets), and that a strum of the shape as written passes the judge that marks the child's strum; it lists the notes as the solution.

The page is the strings view of `music.html`: an instrument switch in the top row (Piano, Ukulele, Guitar), the three directions, five ukulele pieces and four guitar pieces, Listen, My turn and Start again, the two finger options, time in the child's own pace or in time, and the three directions drawn for paper under the stage. The pieces say where they come from: Hot Cross Buns and Mary Had a Little Lamb are traditional and public domain, the Ode to Joy is Beethoven's of 1824, and the open-string pieces and the chord pieces are ours.

## Order to build the rest

1. Guided playing inside a lesson. The lessons page draws a neck or a keyboard and does not play it, so a played question is answered on the music page; this is the row in [gaps.md](gaps.md) that asks for a `phrase` setting, and it now applies to both instruments.
2. The rest of the ukulele year: G7 and two and three chord songs, the Island Strum played, and melodies read from cards.
3. A strummed rhythm as a checked answer. `music.rhythm` works, and the lessons do not use it yet because a bar of even strums trips its warning that it tests the beat rather than the rhythm; a pattern with an up strum in it does not, and wants a lesson of its own.
4. The chord change drill as a game: a minute of changes, counted, the count kept as the child's own record.
5. A left-handed setting on the neck, using the mirror view.
6. The guitar's grade four: open chords and changes, patterns in three and first position notes from
   tab are written (the three grade four rows above); the staff beside tab waits for a guitar clef,
   since guitar music is written an octave above where it sounds and the staff drawing has none.
7. A tuning lesson for the grown-up, with the page sounding each string to match; it needs no microphone.
8. Web MIDI for keyboards, if the owner agrees.
9. Listening, only if the owner decides for it, and only on the terms in this document.

## Open questions

- Whether the ages are right. They follow the sources in direction and are ours in detail.
- Whether "hold them for me" should be on by default at grade one. We have it on, so a first strum succeeds; a teacher might want the finger found first.
- Whether the voices sound like a ukulele and a nylon guitar to someone in a room. They were set by measurement.
- Whether a child reads the quarter turn from card to tab as easily as we expect. It needs a child and a tablet.
