# Reading

Status: proposed, and the first set built in `scratchpad/`, September 2026. This document says why
the reading track looked thin, what current teaching practice asks of reading between the ages of
five and ten, the principles we now build reading lessons to, a scope and sequence for each of the
four grades, the lesson ideas with the drawings each one needs, and the order to build the rest in.
It is written against [tracks.md](tracks.md), which planned the first fifteen reading lessons,
[gaps.md](gaps.md), whose third recommendation is this work, and [curriculum.md](curriculum.md),
whose bar every lesson here has to clear.

The short version is that reading was thin because it was drawn as words in boxes, not because it
lacked lessons. We made the fifteen lessons visual, drew sixteen new pictures that do for reading
what the balance and the number line do for maths, and wrote eighteen new lessons, four or five a
grade, so the track now has thirty three. Every question in them is checked by the verifier for
every value it can take, and every passage and poem was written for the lesson.

## What was thin

We rendered all fifteen reading lessons on `lessons.html` on screen and in print, read every item,
and counted scenes the way [gaps.md](gaps.md) counts them: a scene has a picture when it places a
drawing that is not mostly letters, digits or boxes to write in.

- Pictures were in 18 per cent of reading scenes, against 75 per cent in maths and 96 per cent in
  physics. Eight of the fifteen lessons drew no picture at all.
- Four look sections used the guide and a speech bubble as their picture, which is the page saying
  something rather than showing it.
- The questions named things the shelf draws and drew none of them: Ada's hens and her eggs, the
  children on the bus, two children walking to school, the ducks on the pond, a cat, a dog, a pond
  and a mixing bowl.
- A passage sat in a frame that ended three squares below its last line, so every comprehension
  question had an empty band of paper in it. Wider scenes were worse on screen: a practice block
  lays questions two to a row, each card has room for 19 squares, and a 32 square passage was
  scaled to about 60 per cent, so its 18 pixel prose read at about 11 pixels.
- The sight word lesson taught the, and, was and said, and its questions asked for mat, park and
  bus, which are words chosen by meaning, not words read by sight.
- The alphabet line in the first lesson ringed the letter m for no reason, because m is the
  drawing's default mark and the lesson did not set one.
- The strand stopped at short prose. There was no poetry, no rhyme, no story structure, nothing a
  child reads outside a book (a sign, a recipe, a letter, a timetable, a contents page, a labelled
  diagram), no word parts beyond two letters making one sound, and no way to see that a feeling
  can be read off what a character does.

What was not thin is worth saying too. The answers were all provable, the grown-ups notes were
good, and the passage with numbered lines is the right idea: a question can point at the line the
answer came from.

## What current practice says

We read the sources below in September 2026. The web search budget for the session ran out after
the first few queries, so we fetched the documents we already knew to be authoritative and read
them directly; the Common Core standards pages refused the fetch and are not used here.

The simple view of reading (Gough and Tunmer 1986, refined by Hoover and Gough 1990) treats reading
comprehension as the product of decoding and language comprehension, and Scarborough's reading
rope (2001) draws the same idea as strands that twist together. Our track follows that split: word
reading (sounds, letters, word parts, fluency) and language comprehension (vocabulary, sentences,
stories and the other kinds of writing).

England's reading framework (Department for Education, updated July 2023) asks for systematic
synthetic phonics taught with fidelity to one programme, and for decodable books matched to the
letters and sounds already taught. It says levelled books should not include books where pupils are
expected to guess words "from the first letter, from pictures or context cues, or from syntax".
That decides how our pictures work at grades one and two: a picture checks a sentence after it has
been read and never stands in for reading a word. The same framework treats fluency as progressive
rather than absolute, built by re-reading texts a child can decode, notes that "urging pupils to
read at speed will not increase their fluency", and asks teachers to model how a text is read aloud
and to let pupils prepare a short text or a poem to read aloud or perform. It also recommends a core
set of poems for each year, including rhyming poems, poems strong in alliteration and nonsense
rhymes, because rhyme draws attention to the sounds in words.

The Institute of Education Sciences practice guide on foundational skills for kindergarten to
grade three (2016, revised 2019) makes four recommendations: teach academic language, including
inferential and narrative language and vocabulary (minimal evidence); develop awareness of the
sounds in speech and how they link to letters (strong evidence); teach children to decode words,
analyse word parts, and write and recognise words (strong evidence); and make sure each child reads
connected text every day (moderate evidence). Its guide on comprehension for the same grades (2010)
recommends teaching comprehension strategies (strong evidence) and teaching children to identify
and use a text's structure (moderate evidence), alongside discussion, choosing texts purposefully,
and a motivating context. The guide for grades four to nine (2022) puts decoding of multisyllabic
words, purposeful fluency practice and comprehension-building practices (word knowledge, asking and
answering questions, finding the gist, monitoring understanding) all at strong evidence.

England's programmes of study (2013) give the order within the years we cover. Year one reads words
with the endings -s, -es, -ing, -ed, -er and -est and with contractions, learns to appreciate
rhymes and poems and recite some by heart, and makes inferences "on the basis of what is being said
and done". Years three and four apply "root words, prefixes and suffixes ... both to read aloud and
to understand the meaning of new words", infer "characters' feelings, thoughts and motives from
their actions", prepare poems to read aloud, retrieve and record information from non-fiction, and
learn "the conventions of different types of writing (for example, the greeting in letters ... or
the use of presentational devices such as numbering and headings in instructions)". Years five and
six add fact and opinion and using contents pages and indexes to find information.

Two further practices we relied on without re-reading them this session, and say so plainly. Common
words that do not sound out are now usually taught by sounding out the regular part and learning the
tricky part by heart, which follows Ehri's account of how words become known by sight, rather than
as whole shapes. Vocabulary teaching favours rich work on a few useful words over lists, in the line
of Beck, McKeown and Kucan's Bringing Words to Life (2002), and ordering close words by strength is
a common form of it.

## Principles

These are the rules we built the new lessons to. They extend the bar in
[curriculum.md](curriculum.md) and the two track rules in [tracks.md](tracks.md).

A picture checks the reading and never replaces it. In a picture question every card shows the same
animal or the same place as the sentence, so a child who reads one word and guesses picks a card
that fits half the sentence, and the feedback names the word they skipped. In a first sound or a
missing sound question the word is never written, so the picture is the prompt for saying a word,
which is the skill.

Draw what the words talk about. If the passage has hens in it, the hen is on the page; if the
question is about a bus at the shop, the bus is drawn full, as it was at the shop, so the picture
holds the first number and the reading supplies the second. A picture may set the scene but must
not answer the question: the children in the school passage are named under their drawings, and
only the passage says which one carries the ball.

Give words a shape the way numbers have one. A word ladder changes one letter a rung, a train has
a carriage for each sound or each beat, a prefix and a suffix are bricks laid either side of a root,
and words of one meaning sit along a wedge that grows from a little to a lot. Each of these can
leave one place empty, which is where a provable question comes from.

Show each kind of writing in the shape it really has. A recipe has what you need and numbered steps,
a postcard has a greeting, a message, a sign-off and an address, a contents page has dotted lines to
page numbers, a labelled diagram has a title and a caption, and a poem has lines, rhymes and beats.
A child learns how a kind of writing works by reading the real layout of it.

Make clues countable in black and white. A printed sheet has no colour, so no clue relies on one:
the clue houses differ in windows, doors, chimneys and a cat, the boats in sails, flags and round
windows, and a face carries its feeling in the brows and the mouth.

Fit the question to its card. A question up to 19 squares wide sits two to a row at full size in a
practice block; a wider one, such as a passage, a comic or a story map, is shown on its own so its
words stay at reading size.

Keep every passage original. The passages, poems, notices, recipes and postcards were all written
for these lessons, and none reproduces or closely paraphrases a published book, poem or song.

Let reading lessons live in the worlds. The recipe belongs in the kitchen, the notices and the
timetable in the town, the clue boats in the harbour, and the fourth grade postcards come from the
open sea, the mountains and the island. We did not add these reaches to `src/world/`, which another
piece of work owns this week; the reaches to add are listed under the order to build in.

## Scope and sequence

Each grade reads across five strands: word reading (sounds, letters and word parts), fluency,
vocabulary, understanding what is read (sentences, stories and passages), and kinds of writing. The
tables give what a child learns in each strand and the lessons that teach it, in the order the
journal meets them. Lessons marked new were written in this work; the others are the first fifteen,
redrawn.

The small animals in the picture cards, the street and the comic (a cat, dog, hen, duck, fox, pig
and rabbit), the child figure and the story map's icons are exported from `story.ts` as `critter`,
`kid` and `icon`, and the face as `faceAt` from `speech.ts`, so a new drawing can use them. In the
comic, the story map, the postcard, the sign, the labelled diagram and the instructions, an empty
entry in a list draws ruled lines or an empty box to write in, which the writing track uses. Five
existing drawings changed without renaming any setting: the passage's page no longer ends three
squares below its last line, the sentence strip takes a `size` for large grade one sentences, the
sound boxes and letter cards can now be given their filled letters and ringed cards from a lesson,
and the children drawing can be given names. The passage and the word sort, which lessons used but
the shelf did not show, are now on it.

## What changed in the existing fifteen

Every look section now has a picture, and most questions draw what they talk about: 58 of the
fifteen lessons' 69 scenes carry a picture, 84 per cent where it was 18. Across all thirty three
lessons it is 116 of 164 scenes, 71 per cent, and every lesson has a picture in its first section.
The difference is the word study questions, which are drawn as bricks, ladders and wedges and which
we count as written layouts on purpose, the same way [gaps.md](gaps.md) counts a column sum.

- Letter sounds shows the cat beside its letter cards and rings c on the alphabet, not m.
- Blending sounds shows the dog, its sound boxes and a train with one sound a carriage.
- Words we know teaches said as a word with a tricky part, and its questions now ask for was, said
  and the beside a picture of the cat, the duck and the hen.
- A sentence and a full stop shows one picture with a telling, an asking and a shouting sentence,
  and each question has its own picture.
- Two letters, one sound shows the sailing ship and a train where sh rides in one carriage.
- Clapping the beats shows a butterfly and a rabbit, and its questions are syllable trains with a
  hand-drawn picture of each word.
- What the page says, Who is in the story and Finding the answer draw the hen, the named children,
  the ducks and the bus the passages talk about, and show each passage on its own card.
- Putting the story in order opens with a story map, adds a story map with a stop missing, and draws
  the mixing bowl beside the cake instructions.
- What the word means shows the hills beside the passage about the hill.
- The title shows the book with its heading and the pond the field turned into.
- What it does not say shows the clock at the time Ravi left.
- Fact and opinion has the parrot say each sentence, as the island's parrot does in the journal.
- Reading puzzles opens with the boats at the quay and a note to read against them.

## The order to build the rest in

1. Add the world reaches for the new drawings, one line each in `src/world/`: the kitchen's jug or
   plates for `howto`, the town's market stall or bus for `sign` and `street`, the harbour's boat
   for `moorings`, the woods' path for `storymap`, and the open sea's ship for `postcard`. This puts
   the landmark beside the lesson that draws it, which [gaps.md](gaps.md) found missing in 45 of 60
   pairings.
2. A decodable check for grade one. A code checker that every word in a grade one sentence uses
   only the letter and sound pairs taught by that lesson would make the reading framework's rule
   something the verifier proves. It needs a list of pairs by lesson, and no drawing.
3. Fluency, with a `phrases` drawing that scoops a sentence into the phrases it is read in, and a
   read-aloud page the grown-up marks. Fluency is the one strand with no provable question, so the
   page says the grown-up is the marker, as the writing lessons do.
4. An index and a glossary as a second layout of `contents`, and a map key beside `gridmap`, which
   finish the non-fiction features the curriculum lists for years three to six.
5. A review lesson for reading in each grade. No reading review exists, and the items to draw one
   from now do.
6. Contractions and the -ed and -ing endings for grade one, on `wordbricks`, which the national
   curriculum places in year one and we have not written.
7. A lesson-level `let`, ranked thirteenth in [gaps.md](gaps.md), so one story can run through a
   lesson with the same names and numbers on every page.

## What we did not do

We did not add the world reaches, because `src/world/` belongs to another piece of work this week.
We did not write a reading review lesson for any grade, or any fluency page. Seven items keep their
written layouts: the ai and ay sort, the one-beat and two-beat sort, the word with most beats, the
missing word, the puddle story in order, the title choice and the orphaned bus passage, which is
still in no lesson. The Common Core standards could not be fetched, so no page names a standard.
Phonics still has no sound: the page cannot say a letter's sound, so the letter sounds lesson tells
the grown-up to say it, and recorded speech waits on the decision in [sound.md](sound.md).
