## Preparing lessons before arrival

The marketing map overlay opens near the sample child's current world. Its world, day and lessons
are still those selected by the sample journal. `landingRow` in `engine/ui/paper.ts` is shared by the
camera and preparation; no showcase lesson is substituted for the destination.

After the marketing opening draws and the browser is idle, the destination's lesson data, renderer
and drawings are warmed. Hovering or focusing See the map also warms the overlay module. Explicit
entry bypasses the site's idle/opening wait. Pack and lesson ID identify cached visitor reads, and
failed reads are removed so retry can succeed.

`readingShelf` prepares and measures the selected world's destination sheets behind the map on
hover, keyboard focus or selection. A short dwell avoids rendering for every place crossed by the
pointer. It keeps at most six unused prepared sheets, distinguishes wide and narrow layouts, and
transfers ownership to the reader. Its lifetime scopes the cache to the source pack, reading mode
and level. Navigation has a reserved slot past slow speculative work; obsolete queued work is
dropped, and late results are disposed when the overlay closes. Save-data and slow connections
skip speculative work. Children warm the same destination data and drawings, while their answers
are read through their own authenticated state loader, never through the visitor cache.

The illustrated world entrance can appear while its destination sheets load. The camera waits
for the actual sheets and their measured layout before settling on the day. A small paper status
explains the wait and offers retry after failure. Reduced motion uses the same readiness condition.
The shared nearby-paper loader publishes each completed sheet immediately, limits concurrent
preparation to two sheets, and discards obsolete work. Nearby sheets are prepared beyond the visible
area; distant sheets are released while their heights remain known. A width change clears old paper
and starts preparation together, keeping their order deterministic.

`school/worlds/geography.ts` owns coastlines, fixed `WORLD_SITES`, region labels and map extent.
The shared view selects one subject location from the per-grade progress records; the underlying
records remain separate. Routes to another land use sea travel (or the world's air route).
Model tests cover unique locations, minimum spacing and grade-specific rewards. Browser checks
cover the atlas, child overview, locked-place feedback and map/roll/place navigation.

## What the owner asked for

The first round started from twelve framed thumbnails of each world's horizon in a ring round one piece of land, with "Year 1" to "Year 4" on white labels, grey pencil for every world but two, and a blue box round the current one. We kept the positions, the roads, the keyboard journey and the rule that colour spreads only as far as the child has walked, and changed what was drawn at each position: one country in the sea, each world standing on it as a few of its own shelf drawings.

The second round's comments were three. First, "having the year numbers in the world map is not great, because it feels too far away, should we expand the map so you have to scroll to see the other years instead of seeing them all at once". The owner approved a plan: no year labels on the child's map, a map bigger than the screen that the child pans across, land more than one world ahead left as plain squared paper, and a poster that shows what was walked. Second, "make the lands to be separated by sea and spread out nicely to reflect the real world landscape/map". The sections below take them in that order, after the art direction, which both rounds share.

## The art direction

### A map a child would pin up

The model is the kind of map at the front of a children's book: lands with coasts all the way round, the sea between them, and each place drawn on the land as the few things that stand for it, side on, a little larger than the country would really allow. It is drawn in the shelf's own hand, with the seeded pen, the five markers and 5 mm squared paper under it, and it uses the shelf's drawings wherever a place needs a thing: the harbour is its lighthouse on the point with boats in the bay, the railway is its station and a train on rails, the woods are three stands of firs round a treehouse. Where the map needed a drawing the shelf did not have, we drew it for the shelf, so that a question can use it too.

The rule that keeps it one thing rather than a collage is that there are two materials and they never mix. The lands, the woods, the hills and the coasts are marks the map draws itself, batched into a few paths, in the same pencil and ink as a sheet. The worlds, the vehicles and the creatures are shelf drawings standing on that land. A mark is never a subject and a subject is never texture, which is the line [journal.md](journal.md) already draws between the world's marks and the shelf.

### Coasts, the sea, and what is on the land

Each land's coast is drawn as maps draw it: one firm line, then three lines following it out into the sea, the outer two dashed, and a band of deeper blue along the shore where a painted map puts its shallows. The sea is always washed blue, from the first day, everywhere on the sheet. We tried the sea in pencil like unreached land in the first round, and the first-week map was a grey sketch on white paper that did not read as a place.

On the land, the map draws woods as clumps of trees of one kind (round trees in the country, firs in the great forest and on the mountains' slopes, palms round the island's shore), fells and downs as rows of brows shaded on the side away from the light, ranges of snow-capped peaks round the mountains (drawn from the shelf's own geometry, `rangePeaks` and `peakMarks` in `engine/parts/outdoors/peaks.ts`, at the map's heavier weights: a range is built whole, since a nearer peak hides what is behind it, and each of its marks is filed in the tile its first point falls in, the way a tree belongs to the tile its foot is in. A line a nearer peak stands in front of is left out, so a range needs no fill and the paper and its squares show through the rock; the shadow the snow carries is the one colour a peak has, and in pencil it is left off, 20 September 2026), patchwork fields with their furrows turning from plot to plot, reeds round the lakes, and waves out in the open water. They are laid out in `school/worlds/geography.ts` and drawn a tile at a time as the camera comes near, and they keep clear of the worlds, the roads, the title, the key and the compass.

### The worlds as places

Each world is composed in its own declaration, in its `map` field, as a short list of its own drawings with where each stands, its size, and what it is to the story: the gate the child arrives at, the moment the term waits for, a drawing that moves, or the secret. The meadow is its hills, its apple tree, sheep and flowers round the garden gate, with the kite lying in the grass until the term is finished and flying high over the meadow after. The night hill is a heather hill with the telescope on its top under a patch of night sky full of stars, the one dark thing on the map, and once its moment has happened the rocket is up in that sky on a trail of smoke. The open sea is the ship under a storm cloud with rain falling out of it, and a rainbow once the storm is over. The kitchen is a cottage in the snow with its windows lit, and the laboratory is a hall with rows of windows.

A place is drawn a little larger than its box on the map, growing up and out from the ground the guide stands on, so the worlds are the loudest things on the map. Between the drawings each place scatters a few small marks of its own kind of ground: grass and flowers in the meadow, shells and pebbles at the harbour, ballast by the railway, fallen leaves in the woods, drifts in the snow. A world nobody has composed is laid out from its own horizon, gate at the front and moment beside it (`placeOf` in `school/worlds/places.ts`), so a new world is on the map the day it is added. Every one of the thirty-eight is composed now. What was a special case by id is a field of the composition: the island's place is an isle drawn in the sea (`isle`), is promised on a child's map before it is reached (`promise`) and keeps its own lantern (`ownLamp`), and the night hill's rocket leaves a trail once it is up (`trail` on the spot).

### The ways, and the way the child has come

Every way is drawn in its own kind: a dashed footpath, a road of two lines, rails on sleepers, a dotted line down the river, a dotted sea lane with waves either side, and long dashes through the sky with a small cloud now and then either side, up to the cloud islands. Where a way over land meets water it goes over on a bridge of piers, as the ways do over each land's river and firth; a way from one year's land to the next is a sea lane, since a year ends by sailing to the next land. A way the child has walked is inked, and has a band of yellow highlighter laid under it, the way a child marks a route on a real map, and the day a way is first walked it draws itself along its length as the map opens.

### What the child's work inks, and the colour coming in

Everything [story.md](story.md) says a child's work makes happen has a place on the map. A world reached is in colour. Its moment is a pencil sketch inside the coloured place until the term is finished, and then it is inked and glows; where the moment is something the gate does, the gate waits in its before state, inked, as the harbour's lighthouse waits dark. A landmark a lesson has lit glows. The stamp is pressed at a slant of its own in a corner of the place.

The colour's edge is drawn as colouring rather than as a blur: the reach is a set of circles worked out from the journey, each solid to four fifths of its radius and then a rim of marker strokes, so the frontier looks coloured in by hand. When the map opens, the reach round the child's world grows from where it was before the last lesson to where it is now, on the engine's spring, and whatever the last day of work did inks itself in. A land every one of whose worlds is finished is coloured out to its coasts, headlands and all, and the small islands off its coast are coloured in with it once a world near them is finished. So a year's land is all in colour at the end of that year, and the whole country with its isles at the end of the fourth.

### A lantern for each world

Beside the road into each world stands a lantern on a post, unlit and in pencil until the world's moment has happened, and then lit, with a pool of light round it. The island's moment is its own lantern, so it has no second one. A finished year is three lights along its road, and after four years the road is lit all the way to the lantern at the top of the island.

### A year ends by sailing

Finishing a year is the sail to the next land (18 September 2026). Each land has a jetty where the way out of its year meets the coast (`SAILS` in geography.ts), and on a child's map it stands there from the start of the year with the ship moored at it, facing the next land, and a path down to it from the year's last world, all three in pencil. They are inked on the day every world of the year is finished, and bloom that day as a moment does, which is the rule every moment already follows. A child who has finished a year stays on its land, with the jetty inked and the ship waiting, until the next year's first lesson is finished. From that day they stand in the next year's first world, so their map is the next land, and as it opens on that day the ship comes in over the sea along the way from the last land to the first world's shore and is gone once the child is ashore (`sailOf` in `school/worlds/view.ts`, `paintSail` in `engine/ui/map.ts`). It plays on that day as the day's other doings do, since nothing records what a child has seen, and under reduced motion nothing plays: the map simply opens on the new land. The fourth year's jetty is off the island, toward the far shore. The poster, the site and a grown-up's map do not draw the jetties yet.

### The title, the key and the compass rose

The map has what a real one has, each a drawing on the shelf. The title is lettered on a scroll with its ends rolled, in the western sea beside the first year's land: the child's name, "Maya's map", and under it the day the first world was stamped. The key is a card in the south-east corner of the sea, and the compass rose is in the north-east corner. A child's map, which is one land, has its own title, key and compass for each land, in its sea near the corners of the frame it opens on, clear of its places and ways (`FURNITURE_ON` in geography.ts). The poster has its own title, key and compass in the corners of the sheet, since a poster shows only part of the country.

### Small life, and the rare sight

The map moves only by the motion system [motion.md](motion.md) and [animation.md](animation.md) describe, through `placeArt` and the world player in `engine/motion/world.ts`, and is held to motion.md's budget of four things moving idly at once: the waves drift slowly downwind; the harbour's big boat rocks in the bay once the harbour is reached, and the gull over the open sea bobs once that world is; the volcano smokes from its crater; and the guide keeps its own idle. Everything that moves is paused while the map is closed, off screen or in a hidden tab, and stops under reduced motion.

The map's rare sight is a hot-air balloon from the shelf. Once in a visit, after the map has been open for fifty seconds, it drifts across the child's part of the country and is gone. It is never announced, counted or kept, it does not happen under reduced motion, and a child who was not looking loses nothing.

The country between the worlds has a small life of its own, so the open sea and the empty land are not blank paper while the story stands still: a whale far out that comes up, spouts and goes down, a fishing boat on a slow loop with two gulls behind it, seals hauled out on rocks, bell buoys, the ferry crossing to the ferry town, a cloud and its shadow drifting downwind, and, on a grown-up's map and the site only, dolphins, geese in a V and a tractor standing in a field, since each of those is a world's rare sight. The sea serpent doodled in the margin by the compass is a trial that comes out by removing its line. It is one table, `LIFE` in `school/worlds/life.ts`, beside the riders each kind of way carries (`RIDES`, which the guide's rides and the country's riders in `engine/ui/map.ts` both read), and a child's map, a grown-up's, the apps' backdrops and the site all draw from it through the view (`MapView.life`, each life with its round already sampled), which `paintTerrain` paints. An entry says its drawing, its size, where it may be (at sea this far from every coast, in the sky, on a field, on the way to a place off the run, or by the compass), how it moves (standing, a loop, a shuttle, coming up and going down, or crossing and gone for a while), how many and how far apart, and whether a child's map has it. Where each one lands is worked out from the laid-out map on a seeded sample, keeping clear of every place, way, feature and creature the paper plane spots, so a land, an island or a world added to the geography moves where life lands without the table changing. On a child's map life is only over country the map draws, in colour where the child has been, and what stands there in pencil otherwise; what moves is left out unless its round touches country the child has been to; nothing there is a button, is spotted by the plane or is counted. Under reduced motion what stands is drawn still and what only makes sense moving is left out, which is what the map's snapshots hold.

Everything in it moves on the compositor. A traveller's whole round is written out once as transform keyframes with every value in them, on one HTML element that carries the drawing, its shadow and whatever follows it, and a windmill's sails are lifted into an svg of their own inside a wrapper the compositor turns. We measured why: in Chromium an animation of the individual `translate`, `rotate` or `scale` properties on an outer `<svg>`, or more than one animation changing an `<svg>`'s transform, runs on the main thread, where a single `transform` animation on an `<svg>` or on an HTML element does not, and a `requestAnimationFrame` loop writing transforms costs about ten times as much again. So the world player's float now moves a wrapper round the drawing (lift and turn) and the drawing itself (drift), the sea's wave tiles drift on `transform`, and the site's riders left their per-frame ticker for keyframes. At rest a child's map went from about 90 to 130 ms of main thread a second to about 1, and the harbour from about 740 to 550; what remains on `/home` and `/kids` is the guides' own animations inside their SVGs, which are the next thing to move.

### Words on the map, and where the child is

Names are lettered on the land in the hand face with a halo of paper round the letters, rather than on white labels, and grow a little as the camera draws back. A world not reached has its name in soft ink. Where the child is needs no box: the guide stands at the foot of the child's world in a pool of lamplight, with the creatures that have joined it walking behind, and a note in the teacher's blue pen says "You are here" with an arrow to its feet. The keyboard's focus is a dashed ring in the same pen round the whole place, and the button under each place is never smaller than 48 pixels on screen.

## A child's map, and a grown-up's

### No year numbers for the child

The child's map has places and their names and nothing else lettered on it: no "Year 1" to "Year 4", no taglines under them, no year or term under a world's name, and the words that say where the guide is give the world's name alone. The button a child reads a world by says its name and whether it is finished, here or not reached, without a year. The grown-up's map, which is the journal's "As a grown-up" view, keeps the years: each year's name is lettered across its land with the line from story.md under it (setting out, round the seasons, looking further, over the mountains and the sea), a finished year is underlined with the date it was finished, each world carries its year and term and its note, and the "Every year" zoom out to the whole country is there. Parents need the overview; a child needs the next place.

### A child's map is their own land

A child's map is the land of the year they stand in, edge to edge, with the sea round it (18 September 2026). It opens on the land's rectangle (`LAND_AT` in `school/worlds/geography.ts`, `ownLand` in `school/worlds/overworld.ts`), which at 1,440 by 900 draws a place about 100 to 117 pixels wide, and the camera pans as far as the land and every place of its year reach, the isles in its water among them and for the fourth year the island, with 1,500 units of sea round them. The child can draw back to the whole land and no further. On a phone the map opens closer, on the place the child stands at with the ways leading off it, and the land is what panning reaches; the lands are not shrunk for the phone. Escape brings the child's map back to the land. The land is the year of the world the child stands in, so a child who has finished a year stays on its land until the next year's first lesson opens the next one, which is the sail below. The site's sample child is still framed on the whole country, by `regionOf`, which takes in their world, the one before and the edge of the next.

### Every place of the year is drawn, and what is not open yet is dimmed

Every world and every place a track brings a child to in their year is on a child's map, drawn where it stands, in the pencil the next world already used, dimmed, with its name, and with no way in. So a child always has a land to look at and can see what is ahead in it, and a place they may not go to cannot be mistaken for one they may. Another year's land, with its worlds, places, isles and coasts, is not on it at all. Until 17 September 2026 the land more than one world ahead was plain squared paper, and from then until 18 September the whole country was drawn, every year's worlds dimmed ahead; the owner then asked for each year's land on its own.

The next world is set apart by being lighter than the rest: the places further on are drawn at 0.44 of full strength and their names fainter, the next world at 0.7 with its name in the pencil ink, and the world the child is in is the only one in colour (`engine/ui/overworld.css`). A place with no way in has no landing field for the plane and no way into its roll, and its button is `aria-disabled` and says the place is not reached yet, while staying focusable so a child who tabs onto it hears why.

The island is on the fourth year's map only. A first-year child sees it from the harbour on the harbour's own horizon, as the world's glimpse, which is the story's first sight of where the map ends; the map no longer shows it across the water.

The sea round a child's land shows only the land's own: the rocks in its water, the life in it and the creatures the plane spots there, and the land's own title, key and compass rose in its corners. `landOnly` in `school/worlds/terrain.ts` keeps that ground, and the paper plane on the Worlds tab flies no further than it.

In the child's app this is held as data before it is drawn: `school/worlds/view.ts` works the child's map out as a `MapView` carrying every place's picture and whether it is open, and `engine/ui/map.ts` draws a place that is not reached in pencil. The run's worlds of another year are in the view with nothing to draw and no way in, so every index is the one the journey has, and only the child's own year's places off the run are laid out at all.

The child's page says what a locked place needs rather than the map doing it, through two hooks on `Overworld` (17 September 2026):

- `onLocked({ place, how } | null)` fires when a locked place is tapped, focused or pointed at, with `how` saying which, and with null when the viewer leaves it or the camera moves. While a page takes it, the map opens no card of its own, says no line of its own, and a tap on a locked place does not travel, so the child hears one message and not two.
- `onView(at | null)` gives the page `place(i)`, a place's picture in the page's own pixels, and `controls()`, the map's own buttons and words, whenever the camera comes to rest or the window changes, and null once the camera leaves what it reported. The child's page places the guide's lines and the locked place's note with it.

The arrow keys walk between the places the viewer may travel to, so one press goes on along the run and one goes back, as they did before every place was drawn. A place with no way in is reached by Tab or by panning, and says it is not open as it takes the keyboard. When no place that way is open the map says so, naming the place that is not open yet when there is one drawn that way and saying there is no way that way when there is not. Without that filter a drawn but shut place beside the world the child is in takes the arrow and the keyboard stops walking the run, which is what happened on 17 September 2026 when the dimmed places landed.

### The poster

The poster shows what was walked: the country the child has reached and coloured, and the start of the next world at the edge, where the sheet's own fade takes it back to paper. The year and the dates are in the line along the foot, the title, key and compass are in the sheet's corners, and names are lettered larger than on a screen so they read on a wall. At the end of the first year it is the first year's land with the sea lane going out east toward the second year's and the woods fading at the right-hand edge; at the end of the fourth it is the whole country, four lands in one sea.

## The country

### The shape of it

The worlds stand in a country with real geography, laid out in `school/worlds/geography.ts`. Since 18 September 2026 it is four lands in one sea, one land for each year of the school, with the island the fourth year sails out to in the water between them, and rocks and isles in every land's water. Until then it was two lands split by a strait, with the years running across both; the owner asked for a land a year, so that a child's map is their own land edge to edge and finishing a year is sailing to the next one.

The lands lie clockwise round the sea in the order a child meets them. The first year's is in the south-west: the meadow inland among fields and a windmill, the harbour at the head of a bay on its north coast with a lighthouse on the point, the railway on the flat by its east shore, fells at its west end, a lake in the middle and a river from the fells to the south coast. The second year's is east across the strait: the woods on its western shoulder in a great forest, a lake in that forest from which a river runs down past the kitchen's cottage to an estuary on the south coast, and the town at the head of that estuary, with downs behind it. The third year's is north of the second: the night hill on its southern slope, the sports ground on the flat above, and the laboratory on a sea loch cut into its west side. The fourth year's is the last, in the north-west: mountains through the middle of it, a firth to the south-east where their river comes down, and beyond the firth the open sea and the island.

Each land is about 12,600 by 8,200 units, which is what a child's map frames at a reading zoom, and about 5,000 units of sea lie between one land and the next. The fourth year's land is narrower, since its year goes on out to the island. `LAND_AT` in geography.ts holds each land's box. The whole country, the four lands and their sea, is one sheet of 43,000 by 29,200, with 2,000 units of sea past the outermost coasts. That margin was added on 18 September 2026 when each year's places became its own: the nineteen places of the four years that stand on isles could not all find room in the water between the lands, where one land's isles and the next's competed for the same strip, and the margin gives each land water of its own on its outer side. The fifth year's sheet is taped along the top and moved up with it.

### Where each world stands, and why

Each world stands where it belongs on its own land, three to a land, laid so that every step of the run has a direction of its own for the arrow keys. A year ends by sailing to the next land, so the way into the first world of a year is the sea, and the ways inside a land are its own. The first year walks a path to the meadow, takes the road down to the harbour and the rails across to the railway; the second sails to the woods, walks the path to the kitchen and takes the road to the town; the third sails to the night hill, walks to the sports ground and takes the road to the laboratory; the fourth sails to the mountains, comes down their river to the open sea and crosses to the island. Every land but the fourth carries three kinds of way, and the fourth is the voyage, which is why it carries two.

A place a track brings a child to is one per year it holds lessons in, with that year's lessons for its record (`sidePlaces` in `school/worlds/worlds.ts`, `journey` in `school/worlds/rewards.ts`), so each land has its own marsh, park and painter's hut, and a child's art in two years is two huts on two lands. Its way leaves from a world of its own year: the nearest whose term's place stands on its land, or the nearest of any of them for a way by sea or through the air. A place with no lessons yet, the old tower and the ferry town today, stands once, on the land of the world its declaration names beside it.

Each land keeps a spot for each place its year's lessons bring a child to (`SPOTS` in geography.ts): fifteen or sixteen, on the land by the kind of country each asks for, and on isles in the land's own water for the places that ask for an island, four to six a land and nineteen in all. A place takes the first free spot of the kind it asks for, in its own declaration's word (`site.land.terrain`), in the order the ids sort, so two never take the same one; `spotsOn` in geography.ts does it. The spots were set out by a packing script, `.scratchpad/leftover/map-move/e2-pack.ts`, which judged every candidate with `layoutMap` itself: every way clear of every place's drawings under every choice a family may make, each place 1,850 from every world of the run and 1,550 from every other place, and each isle clearly nearer its own land than any other. The kinds are the packing's preference rather than a promise, so a place that asks for a riverbank stands by the land's river where there was room and elsewhere on the land where there was not. A land has no spare spot. A place added later, or a track's lessons reaching a year they did not before, would find nothing free and be put in a row below the land, in the sea, so a test in `.scratchpad/test/overworld.test.ts` fails then, naming the land and the place, and says to pack the land again with that script and install its table with `e2-install.py` beside it.

The way the story travels between each pair of worlds follows the land: `ROUTES` in geography.ts gives the points each way passes, out of one land's shore and into the next for a crossing. Each way is described for a screen reader by what it passes, worked out from the same ground: "down to the coast", "along the coast", "across the water", "up the path to the door", "up the hill", "up into the mountains", "down the river to the sea", "across the water to the island", and for the sail from one year's land to the next "across the sea", or "back across the sea" the other way.

### Room for every world to come

Every place in the country is kept in `school/worlds/geography.ts` by what it is for rather than by which world stands there. `SLOTS` holds each term's place by its slot (`1.2` for year 1, term 2), so whichever world a family puts in a term stands in that term's place; `ALTS` holds the spot beside a term for a world made to be chosen for it; and `SPOTS` holds, per land, the spots a place a track's lessons bring a child to may stand on, by the kind of country each is. A world added later stands where its term or its kind of spot is without anything already drawn moving: a test adds the garden before the meadow and holds the twelve to not moving an inch, and another holds every place 1,500 units from every other and on land, but for the open sea's term and the cloud islands. A place with no lessons yet stands on the land of the world its `site.land.near` names; every other place's land is the year of its lessons.

- The garden, before the first year, is on the first year's land south of the meadow, where the first path begins.
- The marsh's reeds are by a lake, the park is a seaside bandstand, the ferry town is across a bay, and the painter's hut is by a river, each on every land whose year holds its lessons. The old tower, for history, is on a hilltop; it holds no lesson yet, so it stands once, on the first year's land beside the railway it names.
- The six that were concept scenes are places a track's lessons bring a child to, like the marsh: the long grass on open grass, the fossil cliffs on sea cliffs, the crystal caves in a cave under a land's hills, the cloud islands in the sky over its sea, the coral reef on an isle, and the oasis by the sea. The isles are drawn once a place on them is on the map.
- A world a family chooses instead of a term's own (the winter fair, the farm) stands on the spot kept beside that term's place, so the way runs past where the term's world would have been rather than doubling back. On a map of the whole country, where no family has chosen it, a world to choose stands beside the first term it may be chosen for.
- The fifth year's canal town, observatory and old city are on the far shore, across the northern ocean from the island, which is a second sheet taped along the top of the first. It is in the geography already and is drawn once a run reaches a fifth year, and on a grown-up's map of the whole country, which holds every world.

### What we tried and did not keep

We started from a horseshoe of lands round an inner sea with the island in its middle. It kept the story (the island seen from the harbour) but put the first and third years on its two sides, running up and down the screen, and a year that runs vertically fits a landscape screen badly. We tried the two lands as rounded blobs first; they read as two potatoes, and the capes, the loch, the firth, the long south-west peninsula and a wobble that is stronger in some stretches than others are what made them coasts. We had the island farther out, and from the harbour its smoke was off the top of the screen, so the island moved in to lie just off the harbour's cape. We tried the far lands as pencil for the child, as on the first round's map; with the owner's plan they are paper.

## Travel between the worlds

### What we compared

We sketched five ways for a child to get from one world to another and compared them before building any.

| Way | Controls | The little game | Where it can go | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The train, with a speed lever | One lever: faster, slower, stop | Stop at the platform mark; braking distance is something to see | Only along rails: two of the eleven ways | A lever and a platform for each station; the rails exist |
| A boat, sailing with the wind | Steer, and set the sail to the wind | Tack against the wind; land at a jetty | Only on the water: the open sea, the island, the ferry town, the far shore | Sail and wind physics; tacking is hard at five |
| A hot-air balloon, where height picks the wind | Up and down only; each height has its own wind | Find the height whose wind goes your way; come down on the right field | Everywhere, slowly | No direct steering or speed, so a young child cannot choose where to go |
| The paper plane, flown | A speed lever, steering, and landing on a field | Wind, landing into the wind, stars to catch on the way, creatures to spot | Everywhere the child's map has drawn | One plane, one control scheme; no physics bodies |
| Each way with its own vehicle | A different control for each: a lever, a sail, a burner | All of the above, one each | Everywhere, each way in its kind | Five control schemes to learn and to build |

The train is the most concrete and has the best lever, but it reaches two worlds. The boat teaches the most about wind, but a five-year-old tacking is a frustration, and it cannot cross land. The balloon is the calmest and the most beautiful, and height picking the wind is a real idea, but the child cannot steer it or set its speed, which is what the owner asked for; it stays on the map as the rare sight. Each way with its own vehicle is the most real, and it teaches how places are joined, but it asks a child to learn five ways of driving to go home.

### What we built: the paper plane

We built the paper plane, because it is one way of flying that reaches every world the child can go to, it has all three controls the owner asked for (a speed lever, steering, and a landing), and it carries a small game without a way to fail. It fits the sketchbook: the plane is folded from a page of the same squared book, with the grid showing through its wings, the way the paper bird guide is, and the world's guide rides on it. We also built the animation half of the last idea, with no controls: when the child walks the guide along a way with the arrow keys, the guide rides that way's vehicle, a train along the rails, a bus on the roads, a narrowboat down the river, a ship across the sea from one year's land to the next and the hot-air balloon up to the cloud islands, and walks the footpaths.

The plane flies from the Fly button on the map, or the P key (F shows the map full screen on the Worlds tab). Its lever has four notches, Land, Slow, Cruise and Fast. The plane takes up each notch's speed on a spring, and climbs when it goes faster and sinks when it slows, the way a glider's lift follows its speed, so height is never a control of its own. It turns while a direction is held. The wind blows from the west, stronger over the sea and turning a little from place to place, and carries the plane more the higher it flies. The camera follows it, leading it the way it is going.

The little game has four parts, and none of them is counted:

- Landing on the right spot. Each world the child can go to has a landing field under its name, a mown strip with a windsock at its end; on the water it is a ring of buoys. With the lever on Land over a field the plane comes down and rolls to a stop, and the guide steps off into that world. Coming in against the wind, which the windsock shows, is a short, soft landing, and the words say so; with the wind behind it is a long roll. Anywhere but a field the lever on Land only brings the plane down to skim low over the country, and it flies on.
- Stars along the way. Stars are strung along the road to a world worth flying to: a world the child's learning has just opened the way to, or home to the world they are in, or back to the one before. Flying through one makes it go.
- Spotting creatures. A whale in the strait and another in the fourth year's water, an eagle over the mountains, a fox in the forest, rabbits in the meadow's fields, a heron at the marsh lake, hares on the fourth year's land, crabs by the third year's loch, a seal off the first year's west cape, a duck on the forest lake and sheep on the downs, each where it lives and where a child's map reaches. Flying low over one rings it in the teacher's pen, writes its name beside it and says it aloud. It is drawn only while flying.
- The edge of the paper. Near the edge of what the child's map has drawn, the plane turns back by itself and the words say "The paper is blank past here. It fills in as you go."

It keeps the game rules [arcade.md](arcade.md) sets out. There is no clock: the plane flies as long as the child likes. There is no score: the stars and the creatures are not counted, and nothing the flight does is recorded, as the secrets are not. There is nothing to fail: nothing can be crashed into, the plane cannot come down except on a field, and Stop or Escape lands it at the nearest world.

It respects how the journal works. The road to a new world is still opened only by learning, as `school/worlds/rewards.ts` works it out from the record: the plane may land only at worlds the child has reached and at a world whose way their learning has opened, the journal gives it that list, and the land it can fly over is the land the child's map has drawn, so it never skips a lesson or shows blank land early. When the child's learning has just opened the way to a new world, the guide waits at the world before it and the map says the way is open, so arriving is something the child does, by walking the road or by flying.

It is built on the engine in `src/engine/`: the game in `engine/motion/plane.ts` is a pure step on the engine's fixed loop at sixty steps a second, reading one `Pad` from `pad.ts`, so the arrow keys, the on-screen buttons, a drag on the map and a gamepad are one set of controls; speed, height, the roll-out after touchdown and the camera's follow are the engine's closed-form springs; and the take-off and arrival use the journal's timelines. It needs no rigid bodies, so it does not use `bodies.ts`. The plane, the windsock and the cloud are new drawings on the shelf, each with a catalogue entry of two takes and a shelf line, and the cloud's slow drift is declared in `src/art/animation.ts`. Under reduced motion the loop does not run: each press flies the plane a little way, a fifth of a turn and then straight on, and it is drawn once where it got to, so it moves only while the child presses. The steering and stop buttons are 60 pixels and the lever's notches 48, and Stop and the lever are buttons a screen reader reads, with the flight's words in a live region.

`engine/motion/__tests__/plane.test.ts` holds it to its rules: it climbs with speed and comes down only on a field, with the lever down over a field it lands and says whether it came in against the wind, it cannot fly out over the blank paper, steering turns it and the wind carries it more the higher it flies, nothing in it is a score, it catches stars and spots a creature only when low, and the same hands give the same flight.

### What to try

Open the Worlds tab at the whole map, or zoom out past the year to it, and press Fly or P. The left and right arrows steer, up and down move the lever, Space goes faster, and Escape lands at the nearest world. Follow the stars to the next world and put the lever on Land over the mown strip under its name; flying in from the east, against the wind, the landing is short. Fly low over the harbour's bay to find the whale. Fly east past the edge of what the map has drawn and it turns back. On a phone the steering buttons and the lever are under the map, and dragging across the map steers. To ride a vehicle, walk with the arrow keys from the harbour to the railway, and the guide goes along the rails by train; walk on from the railway and it sails to the next year's land.

## The story at the scale of the whole map

story.md is the series bible and stays so. This section adds what a whole map needs that a chapter does not.

### The map is drawn as the child goes

The premise is that the journal is a map drawn as the child goes, and the map makes that literal by colouring the land they have walked, one world at a time. Every world and subject place remains visible in pencil until its way opens, with the next world set apart from the more distant ones, so the child can see the whole country without mistaking an unavailable place for somewhere they may go. Its title says whose it is and the day it was begun, and it is the thing a family will print.

### A thread across four years: the lamplit road

The destination is the lantern at the top of the island, and the reason to go is visible rather than told. Every world has a lantern by the road into it, lit on the day its moment happens, so the island's lantern is the last of twelve rather than a surprise. A child sees the lanterns they have lit behind them when they pan back along their road, and from the harbour in the first year they see the island's smoke across the water; a grown-up sees the whole string. It counts nothing a child can see as a number, and it can never go out, because a moment once happened stays happened.

### The cast on the map

The cast in story.md grows up across the years by where it is and what it is doing, and the map shows the part of it that stands in the places, close in: the gull over the harbour and again over the open sea, the family of crabs on the island's beach, the lighthouse on the harbour's cape, and the secrets. The creatures a lesson has brought walk behind the guide. The creatures a flight spots are the country's own, and they stay where they live.

### What the child finds at the edges

The edges of a child's map are the edges of what they know, and the sea goes on past them. The worlds' secrets are drawn only when the camera is close, so they are found by looking and not by reading, and nothing records that one was found. At the end of the fourth year, from the lantern at the top of the island, the far shore is across the northern ocean, and that is where a fifth year goes, on a new sheet taped along the top, which is the answer to story.md's open question about what the map does after the fourth year.

### Moments a parent will want to see

A parent will want to see the first stamp, with the title's "Begun on" date; each world's moment, with its day in the grown-up's note; the first time a way is walked, drawn in along its length; the day a land is finished, when it is coloured to its coasts; and the last lantern. Each of these is a picture a family can print, and the poster below is the one we have built.

### How the map grows

The map grows without anything already drawn being drawn again. Every world has its place in the country, by its term's slot or its own site, so adding the garden, the fifth year or a subject's place draws a new place on land that is already there, and the country's coasts, rivers and woods do not move. ## Parents: the map at a glance, customising it, and the poster

### What a parent sees

A grown-up sees the whole country, with the child's colour on it and the land ahead in pencil, the year names lettered on each land, and, beside each world, its note: the lessons finished of the term's lessons, which is the plan's own count, the day it was stamped and by which lesson, each landmark lit and by which lesson, who walks with the guide and since when, and the moment's day. At a glance a parent can read where the child is (the guide and its note), how far through the term (how far the colour has come round the child's world and along the road ahead), what is finished (lit lanterns, underlined years, lands coloured to their coasts, and the highlighter), and what the year holds next. None of this is a score: nothing on the child's map counts, and the counts are in the grown-up's notes. A grown-up can fly the paper plane too, over the whole country.

### A grown-up's own map, and a look over a page

Built 21 September 2026, from the owner's ask that grown-ups explore the whole overworld and every world in it with nothing locked, in the map-only view the child has. The grown-ups' app has a Map place in its bar, at `/map`: the school as written, the whole country with every world of every year and every place off the run drawn and open, the years lettered, pan and zoom to the sea's edge and no further (the cover floor and the fence the child's map has, so the sea reaches every edge of any window), and nobody on it: no guide, no "You are here", no colour that follows a record, each place's note saying its year and term and how many lessons it holds. It reads the family's pack and no child's record, so it works with no child added; a child's own map on it is a follow-up the view already allows. Going into a world opens its roll as written (`school/worlds/written.ts`, `engine/ui/reading.tsx`): every day of its year, numbered rather than dated, with each sheet drawn as the roll comes near it at the level the lesson is written, with the answers and the notes for grown-ups, nothing lit and no secret shown, since nothing was done; the world's trail as a place is not drawn for a written year yet, so the way in is map to roll and back. Where the grown-up is goes in the address, so back and a shared link work.

The same map and roll open over a page as a look (`engine/ui/overlay.tsx`), with the address after the `#`, focus held and given back, and nothing recorded: Explore's lesson page has "See it as a child sees it", which opens the lesson at the card's level in its world with nothing filled in, and "Open on the map", which goes to the screen; the site's opening has "See the map", which opens the sample child's map with every world open and each world's roll with the sample's record. The calendar and the journal are still to come as callers. The children's map runs the same code, and it was this work that found the map going in again 0.8 s after a way out, since a stopped view still fired its settle; `CanvasView.stop` now drops it.

### What a parent may change

The map follows journal.md's choices exactly and adds none. A parent chooses which world each term is in, and the chosen world stands at its own place in the country, or beside the term's place when it has none; they choose its guide, its ground's colour and its weather, and the map draws what they chose; and the child's name is the one the product already stores. A parent may not colour the map, move a place, rename a world or change what inks what, because those are the story's and the record's.

### The map as a keepsake

The poster is one child's map at the end of a term or a year, printed on real paper and meant to be framed. It is the map as a page on the Worlds tab, `world.html?place=map&form=page`, and it draws the map with the same code the journal does, `paintTerrain` and `paintMap` in `engine/ui/map.ts`, for a sample child at whatever point in the school the tab's drawer names, from a few weeks into the first year to the end of the fourth, onto a single landscape sheet of 5 mm squared paper, cropped to what was walked and the start of the next world, with the sheet's own title, compass and key in its corners and a line along the foot: "Maya's map at the end of year 4. Every world of every year, all twelve walked, from 3 August 2026 to 26 February 2030", the dates being the first stamp and the last moment in the record. It prints with the browser's own print, as a lesson does, and the print check holds it to exactly one page on every paper size. It is drawn in the browser, from the record the device already has, so nothing about the child is sent anywhere to make it. On the poster the map stands still, and its buttons and the "You are here" note are left out.

We recommend A3 landscape, and the page opens at it. A3 is the size a family frames: 30 by 40 centimetre frames with a mount take it, and at A3 the world names, lettered larger on paper than on a screen, can be read at arm's length. A3 and A4 have the same proportions, so the same composition prints on A4 at home as a proof. US families have Tabloid, 11 by 17 inches, as their A3, and Letter as their A4, and both are offered. The foot line and the title are the only words, and the only name on it is the child's.

## More to see on the map, for a child and a grown-up

Status: proposed, and not built, 13 September 2026.

### A world's moment, on the map

A world's moment now happens on the map as well as on the roll. When the map opens and the latest day in the record is the day a world's last lesson was finished, the camera comes to rest on that world and its moment plays there: the lighthouse's lamp comes on and its beam sweeps the bay, the kite lifts out of the grass to its place over the meadow, and the lantern by the road into the world lights with a pool of light round it. Nothing records that it has been seen. It plays each time the map opens until another lesson is finished, which is the rule the map already follows for a stamp pressed and a way drawn in, so a child who missed it sees it the next time, and a grown-up can open the map with the child to watch it together. Under reduced motion the world is simply drawn with its moment inked. We considered playing it once and keeping a flag, and did not, because a flag is a record of what the child looked at, which [story.md](story.md) declines for the secrets for the same reason.

One rule changes before any of this is built. A world's moment has waited for every lesson of its term in every track, so a family that does maths and reading but has coding off never sees a moment inked, a lantern lit or a land coloured to its coasts, and their poster says that nothing has been walked. The rule is being changed so that a world's moment follows the lessons in the family's plan for that term: the tracks the family has on, and the lessons the plan puts there. The rest of the rule stays as story.md sets it out. The moment is worked out from the record each time, and a change to the plan after a moment has happened must not take it back, since nothing a child has earned is taken away.

### This week on the map

On the grown-up's week, beside each child, a small crop of that child's map shows what the week's lessons did, marked in the teacher's blue pen: the way walked into a new world, the stamp pressed, each landmark lit with the lesson and the day that lit it, the creature that joined the guide, and how far the colour came round the child's world. Under it are two or three sentences in the words the grown-up's notes already use, such as "Maya arrived at the town on Monday, with Grams and kilograms. The clock tower lit on Thursday, by Quarter past." With two children there is a crop for each. It gives a parent the week as places and things to talk about with their child rather than as a percentage, and a reason to look at the map every week rather than only at the end of a year.

It could brush against the refusal of streaks, since a week is a run of days. It stays clear because it says only what happened, never which days nothing did: it has no row of days, no comparison with the week before and nothing that counts days in a row. It is on the grown-up's side only, and the child's map is unchanged by it. Nothing is stored for it. It is the map worked out from the record up to the end of the week before and again up to today, and the difference between the two. It needs that difference, a small pure function beside the one that works out the map, a still crop drawn the way the poster draws its sheet, and sentences built from the notes the grown-up's map already writes. It needs no new drawing and no new data. We think it is about two days.

### Subject places on the child's map

The four places a subject brings a child to whose lessons exist, the marsh, the park, the ferry town and the painter's hut, stand on the child's map once the child has done something there. After the first music lesson is finished, a footpath runs from the harbour along the promenade to the park, and the bandstand stands there in colour with its stamp; the child can walk there or fly there, and going in opens the park's own roll of that child's music lessons. When the last music lesson is finished, the band's notes go up over the park on the map as well as on the roll. The marsh comes after the first of the science lessons it holds, the painter's hut after the first art lesson and the ferry town after the first Spanish lesson. A lesson finished counts in both places, the term's world and the subject's place, since a lesson keeps its one record. The old tower waits for a history track, and until there is one it is not on the child's map. Today these four can be seen only on a grown-up's visit, with a made-up record, so four worlds that are built and have real lessons behind them are not yet places a child can use. This would make them places, and it lets the map grow sideways as well as along the road.

It could brush against the refusal that nothing gates a lesson, since a place appears because of work done. It stays clear because the place follows the lessons rather than leading them: it appears after a lesson is finished, it is never needed to open one, and every lesson stays on the roll whether or not the child ever goes there. A place once stamped stays stamped, and nothing about it is counted on the child's map. It needs a kind of place on the map that stands off the run, with a footpath from the world it stands beside, for which the geography already keeps a spot and a neighbour; the child's own record passed to a subject place, which a visit does not use today; the rule for where a child may travel extended to these places; and a composition on the map for the marsh, the ferry town and the painter's hut, which are laid out from their horizons today while the park is composed. We think it is three to four days, most of it the map's new kind of place.

It needs a small scheduler shared with the balloon, kept to motion.md's budget. About a day.
3. From a button on the map, or at the end of a term, the camera travels the child's road from their first world to today, resting at each world while its moment plays and its lantern lights, and ends at "You are here". A grown-up can sit with the child and watch it, and it shows how far the child has come without a number. The child can stop it at any point, nothing in it is timed, and under reduced motion it is a series of stills. It needs a timeline over the travel the map already does, and no new drawing. About a day and a half.
4. When a world's moment happens, the grown-up's page shows a card of it: the world's horizon with the moment inked, its line, the child's name and the date, to print or to save to their own device. It is the picture a family wants to show someone. It has no share button and nothing is sent anywhere, since it is made in the browser as the poster is. It needs a still of the world's horizon with the moment's own settings, which the parent's pages already draw for their pictures of a world. About a day.
5. The pencil and highlighter the child uses on the roll work on the map too, so they can circle the island, draw a dragon in the sea or mark where they saw the whale, and their marks are on the poster. The marks stay on the device, as the roll's working does, and nothing asks where the child lives or offers to mark it. It needs the working kept against the map's own coordinates, the tools on the map and the poster drawing the marks. About two days, and it waits on the open question in [journal.md](journal.md) about where a child's working lives.
6. A child taps a world they can go to and the stars string out from the plane to its field, so a five-year-old can choose where to go without steering the whole way, and long dashed currents out at sea carry the plane faster where they run. There is still no clock and no score, and the stars are not counted. It needs a tap that sets where the stars lead and currents in the map's wind. About a day.
7. In the grown-up's panel, beside each world a term may take, a line says which of its landmarks stand beside that term's lessons and which stand beside none, and the map shows the world in the term's place before anything is kept. The winter fair in the third year's winter term, for example, has no lesson today that reaches any of its landmarks. It is on the grown-up's side only. It needs the matching of reaches to a term's lessons that the world test already does, and a chosen world standing in its term's place on the map. About a day.
8. Of the creatures their lessons have brought in a world, the child picks the one that walks right behind the guide, on the map and beside today's sheet. It is the one small choice journal.md asks whether a child should have. Nothing is bought and nothing is taken away, since the others still walk, and the choice is a setting rather than a record of anything the child looked at. The creatures have no lines, so none becomes a companion. It needs a setting for each child and a picker made of pictures. About a day.
9. On the grown-up's plan for the coming week, beside each lesson, the landmark that will stand beside it and its line, with a sentence written by a person for something to do at home, such as a real clock on the day the clock tower stands beside quarter past. It could brush against the refusal of currency if a child read it as a list of things to earn, so it stays on the grown-up's side and the child's map shows nothing of what is ahead. It needs the reaches worked out for planned days, which the journal already does for today, and a line for home written on each reach. About a day, and the writing.
10. The board on the painter's hut, on the map and in the hut's own world, fills with small copies of the paintings the child made with the painting tool, so it is the one place on the map made by the child's hand. The pictures stay on the device and are never counted on the child's surface. It needs the painter's hut on the child's map, from the section above, the decision about where a child's paintings are kept, and the hut's board taking pictures. Two to three days after the subject places.

## Performance

The first round's numbers were measured in the development server on a 1440 by 900 window; the second round's on a 1440 by 1000 window, both headless, on a machine other work was loading heavily (load averages of 8 to 20), so the figures move between runs by a few hundred milliseconds and we give ranges.

The map adds nothing to the time the journal takes to open, because it is still built the first time it is opened. Before the second round the journal opened for grade two at mid-year in a median of 1,153 ms (1,092 to 1,652), with the old map as a control at 1,148 ms. After it, the same page opened in a median of 1,378 ms in the development server at a heavier load, against 1,391 ms for the control in the same session, and in 1,104 ms (1,047 to 1,237) from a built copy of the page, which no other work's edits could reload mid-run.

Building the map when it is first opened took 44 to 73 ms when it was measured, against 12 to 23 ms for the old framed map and 24 to 53 ms for the first round's map. Most of it is the coasts of the lands and their isles, smoothed and wobbled, and the bridges and phrases worked out from them. Two changes brought it down from 74 to 122 ms: finding which side of a coast is the sea from the way round its ring goes, rather than testing a point against every coast, and drawing the edge of a child's known land as a soft fade rather than a rim of hatched marker strokes, which is kept for the colour's edge.

Drawing every unavailable place was measured again on 17 September 2026, on the same page and the same machine, before and after the change: a grade 1 child's map is ready in 454 ms against 433 ms, and 1,128 ms against 1,049 ms with the CPU slowed four times, and it holds 21,964 elements against 5,789, with 31 places drawn against 6. The time barely moved because the map still paints only what the camera can see, a few milliseconds at a time, and the elements are what the child pans over rather than what the first frame draws. Laying the country out and working out its coasts, which every map does whatever it draws, are 0.6 to 2.5 ms and 5.7 to 12.6 ms.

While the camera pans a child's map, the page costs about 250 to 280 ms of main thread a second once its tiles are painted, and 370 to 510 ms while it is still painting the land it is panning onto, against 190 to 260 ms for the old framed map in the same runs and 215 ms for the first round's map. Drawing a child's coasts only where their map knows the land saved some of that. Flying the paper plane from the kitchen to the woods and landing costs 139 to 177 ms of main thread a second, of which the flight's own work, the game's steps, the drawing and the camera, is 0.09 to 0.12 ms a frame. The camera keeps its zoom while flying: we had it draw back as the plane climbed, and every change of zoom drew the whole map again, at 864 ms of main thread a second. Under reduced motion no animation runs while flying.

The map behind the apps' pages was measured again on 14 September 2026, from built copies of the apps served on one origin with compressed, hashed files, in headless Chrome on a machine other work was loading (load averages of 10 to 18), cold unless we say otherwise. Before, the site's opening at 1,440 by 900 showed its sheet on a plain white box and drew the map at 825 ms. At four times slower CPU it drew the map at 2.7 s behind one task of 1.5 to 1.6 s, and with that CPU on a slow 4G connection (150 ms round trip, 1.6 Mbps down) at 9.8 s, or 10.5 s on a phone. Sign-in and the family's page drew theirs at 1.2 to 1.25 s at four times slower CPU, behind one task of 700 to 820 ms, and at 6.2 to 6.8 s on slow 4G.

Five changes brought that down. The map's box is squared paper from the first stylesheet, so no frame of it is white. `tools/first-view.ts` works out the sample child's journey and the site's words when the apps are built, so the site's opening map never parses the corpus, which loads once the map is drawn. `engine/ui/backdrop.tsx` shows a snapshot of the framed map from `engine/ui/snapshots/`, drawn in Chrome by `tools/scripts/map-snapshots.ts` with the same painters and placed by the same camera arithmetic the live map takes (`aimCamera` in `engine/ui/snapshot.ts`), and the live map fades in over it. The map's code carries no notation and no lesson renderer. And the first frame is painted in steps (`Overworld` with `steps`, since 16 September 2026): the country, then the places, then what the camera sees a few milliseconds at a time, each in a task of its own, with the drawings that travel the country starting when the page is next idle.

After them, the site's opening shows its snapshot with its first content: at 114 ms unthrottled, 230 ms at four times slower CPU, and 2.2 s on slow 4G, where the squared paper is there at 0.66 s. The live map is drawn at 240 ms, 0.6 s and 6.2 s. Sign-in and the family's page show their snapshots at about 0.3 s at four times slower CPU and draw the live map at 0.7 to 0.8 s, and their longest task fell to 130 to 160 ms. The steps still over 50 ms are laying out the country and its coasts (`terrainOf`, 70 to 100 ms at four times slower CPU) and painting the land and sea (`paintTerrain`, 130 to 170 ms), which would need the terrain built ahead of time and the land painted a tile at a time. On the site, parsing the corpus for the pictures below the opening is still one task of about 0.9 s at four times slower CPU once the map is drawn, until the site reads a built pack. The Worlds tab, which now verifies an item only when it is read, draws its maps at 1.0 to 1.7 s, against 6.3 to 7.1 s before, and at 4.2 to 6.1 s at four times slower CPU, against 26.7 to 29 s.

We measured the four lands' cost on 18 September 2026, in Node on the development machine, unthrottled, the median of fifteen runs. Building a child's map (`mapViewOf`) takes 16 to 22 ms in each year, and a grown-up's whole country with every place of every year 58 ms. Laying the run out with every place off it takes 21 ms, most of it bending each place's way round the others, which is why a child's map lays out only its own year's places, in 4 to 7 ms; before that change a child's map took about 40 ms. Working out the ground takes 5 ms. At the end of the work the child's map screen's script is 368,258 bytes of its 380,000 (358,363 after the four lands' first stage), the child's page 104,067 of 125,000, the site's first script 90,254 of 100,000, the opening map's code 1,175,914 of 1,850,000 and the site's data 17,480 of 25,000. We have not measured the map in a browser since the four lands.

The map looks and moves as it did. Comparing the live map on the built site before and after, 98 to 99 percent of pixels are identical and fewer than 0.02 percent differ by more than 48 of 255. Sign-in differs by a thin, even halo along its strokes on 0.8 percent of pixels, with identical markup and styles, whose cause we have not found. With motion allowed the travelling drawings still move, and under reduced motion nothing does. We also tried caching the palette in `readTokens` for the length of a task; measured on the Worlds tab against the same build without it, it made no difference, so we did not keep it.

## What is built, and what is sample

`school/worlds/geography.ts` is the country: its lands, rivers, lakes, woods, hills, peaks, fields, drawings, creatures to spot, where each world stands, the spots kept for chosen worlds, and the ways between them. `school/worlds/overworld.ts` lays the run out on it and works out a child's frame. `school/worlds/terrain.ts` makes the geography into coasts, the ground round each world, the bridges and the phrases, and works out how far the child has come and what their map knows. `engine/ui/map.ts` draws the map: the country, the sea and the reach in `paintTerrain`, and the places, the ways, the labels, the furniture, the guide and its vehicles in `paintMap`, and the worlds to come in `paintFuture`. Each world's declaration holds its composition, `school/worlds/places.ts` the year names and the composition for a world nobody has composed, and `src/world/future.ts` the worlds to come. `engine/motion/plane.ts` is the paper plane's game and `src/world/fly.ts` runs it on the page. `engine/ui/overworld.css` styles the map and `src/styles/flight.css` the flight. The poster and the worlds to come were the two pages of the first round, `poster.html` and `worlds.html`; they are now forms of the Worlds tab, `src/pages/world-poster.ts` and `src/pages/world-to-come.ts`, and their old addresses open the same views there ([journal.md](journal.md), "The Worlds tab"). The drawings added over both rounds are the map title, the map key, the hot-air balloon, the canal lock, the narrowboat, the paper plane, the windsock and the cloud in `src/art/travel.ts`, the hall, the washing line and the bandstand in `src/art/story.ts`, and the heron and the bird hide in `src/art/nature.ts`, each with its catalogue entry and shelf line. `test/overworld.test.ts` holds the country, the places for every world, a child's map and frame, the year names' contrast, the compositions, the colour of finished lands and the worlds to come; `test/world.test.ts` holds the run, the ground and the reach; `engine/motion/__tests__/plane.test.ts` holds the flight.

The old map, drawn as framed pictures in a ring, is gone: `src/world/map-before.ts`, `src/world/terrain-before.ts`, the ring layout in overworld.ts, the `?map=before` switch and the styles only it used in journal.css.

The child, Maya, and her record are the journal's sample; the poster makes up a record the same way for any term's end. The worlds to come show the lessons their paths hold, and a world whose lessons are not written yet is walked empty, with nothing standing in for them.

## Open questions

- Whether the places a subject visits should be on a child's map from the first lesson of that subject, or from the first time they visit, and whether their footpaths should start at the nearest world or at the world the child is in.
- Whether a child should be able to fly to an unavailable world once a grown-up has opened it for them.
- Whether the poster should be something a parent orders printed and framed, which would send a picture of the child's map to a printer, and so to a third party; we would not, and the poster as built is printed at home.
- Whether a child should see the lantern thread explained anywhere, or only find it. We think only find it.
