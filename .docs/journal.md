# Journal

Status: proposed, and built as a working page, September 2026. This document answers the owner's question about what a child actually opens, argues three whole answers against each other, recommends one, and sets out the model for themed worlds and a parent's customisation of them. The recommendation is built on the scratchpad's Worlds tab, `world.html`, with the world model in `school/worlds/` (its painters still in the scratchpad's `src/world/`), so the argument can be checked against the thing rather than only read. A second pass the same month took the journal from three sample worlds to nine, three whole years of them, with landmarks that reach into the lessons of their term, fifteen new drawings, and a roll that paints only what is near the camera, so that it opens in about a second whatever the grade. A third pass split the wash rule so that a night can be dark and a dusk can glow above the horizon, drew the fourth year's own three worlds and thirteen drawings for them, and added the map of every world at the top of the journal's zoom, with the story the worlds tell and what a child's work makes happen on the map, which [story.md](story.md) sets out as a series bible. A fourth pass redrew that map as an illustrated map, one country in the sea with each world standing on it as a few of its own drawings, which [overworld.md](overworld.md) sets out with the worlds proposed to come after the twelve and a poster of a child's map to print. It is written against [product.md](product.md), [curriculum.md](curriculum.md), [tracks.md](tracks.md), [parents.md](parents.md) and [data-model.md](data-model.md), and it takes the data model's recommendation and the store in [db.md](db.md) as given. A fifth pass built the eleven worlds proposed in [overworld.md](overworld.md) as worlds in the journal, with a way for a grown-up to visit any of them, the winter fair and the farm as a family's choice for a term, and a roll for a world whose lessons are not written yet; "The eleven round the run" below says what changed, and overworld.md has each world's identity. A sixth pass added a second form of the roll beside the canvas, a page that scrolls from a world's arrival down to today and prints, which the section on the journal as a page describes. A seventh pass joined the journal, the journal as a page, the map poster and the worlds to come into that one tab, with the map full screen when it is wanted, which the section on the Worlds tab describes.

The owner asked for three things at once: an answer to what replaces the lessons tab in the product, a decision about the journal or infinite canvas idea, and themed worlds that parents can customise, with beauty as the top bar. The three turn out to be one question, because where a world can be drawn depends on what shape the child's surface is, and the shape depends on how a five year old finds their place in it.

## What exists, and what each surface is for

`lessons.html` is a developer's surface for reading any of the 142 lessons in either output, and it is the harness the print guard drives. It stays as it is. Nothing a child would open looks like it: a rail of every lesson in the corpus is the view of someone who writes them.

`journey.html` is the year seen from above. It lays one grade on one sheet of squared paper, groups nine units into three terms drawn as islands, runs a path through the lessons with the other tracks hanging off it, and draws each lesson as a mark, a card or its pages depending on distance. It is a map of the curriculum: what is finished, what is locked and why, what comes next, and where the other tracks sit. Its second mode, the lesson laid out as a place, puts one lesson's sections side by side on the paper with room to write between them. That mode is the seed of the journal, and building on it taught us two things. The first is that working written around a sheet is the most natural thing on the canvas and the thing a child would miss most in a book. The second is that working kept in world coordinates is fragile: when anything above it changes size, the working stays behind. The page was deleted on 17 September 2026, once the map of worlds had replaced it.

The art shelf is about 213 drawings in 29 categories. For worlds the useful parts are the nature and story categories, the journeys shelf, the cast of five hand-drawn animals, and the hand-drawn objects and settings. The guides page is seven designs for the character that asks the questions, all drawn in the same 60 unit box with the same poses, which means any of them can live in any world. `play.html` holds ten activities and `music.html` a keyboard and guided playing; a journal has to hold both, and neither prints.

### One canvas at two distances, or two things

The map and the journal look like the same idea at two zooms, and we think they are two things that share one grammar. The test that separates them is a lesson the child skipped. On the map it is still there, a node on the path with its lock and its reason, because the map shows the plan. In the journal there is no paper for it, because the journal shows what was done, in the order it was done, with what the child wrote. Put them on one canvas and one of two things happens: the child's book fills with locks and a future it has not reached, or the parent's plan fills with scribbles and loses its shape.

What they share is everything below the content: the camera and its flights, the squared paper that stays one device pixel wide at any zoom, levels of detail that each answer one question, labels that hold their size far out, and the step above the year handing over to a different drawing rather than zooming further. The journal reuses the map's `CanvasView` and camera maths unchanged. The map stays the parent's planning surface, which is where [parents.md](parents.md) already puts the plan, and the journal becomes the child's.

## What a child opens: three answers

Each answer is described for the same child, a five year old in grade one on a Monday in November, with the same six questions: what they do on a Monday, where today's work appears, what happens to yesterday's, how they find their place again, what a parent sees, and what it costs to build.

### A. One infinite canvas

The journal is a single sheet of squared paper with no edges. Each lesson lands on it as a cluster of sheets, the work stays where it was done, and zooming out becomes the year.

On Monday the child opens the canvas where they left off and today's lesson has been placed as a new cluster near Friday's. Where exactly is a layout policy the canvas has to own: along a path, in a spiral, on an island per unit or per world. Yesterday's sheets stay where they were with whatever was written round them, and the appeal is real: the sheet from three weeks ago is in the place the child did it, and the space itself becomes a memory. A five year old finds their place with a home button, a minimap and the guide standing at today. They need all three, because with two free directions a child who drags the paper can end up on blank paper in any of eight directions, and the map already needs a pointer at the edge of the screen to say where the content went. A parent sees the same canvas and zooms out.

The cost is the highest of the three. The layout policy for an unbounded plane is new work, culling has to run in two directions, a world's art has to cover a plane of unknown extent without colliding with sheets placed by that policy, and working has to be anchored to the sheet it belongs to rather than to the plane, which the map's ink does not do.

### B. A book with pages that turn

The journal is a book. Each day is a spread, the lesson on the right page and the world on the left, and the child turns pages.

On Monday the book opens at today's spread. Today's work is a new page at the end, yesterday's is one turn back with its working on it, and a five year old finds their place the way they do in a real book: it opens where the ribbon is. Nothing can be lost, because a book has two directions and one of them is a page count. A parent sees the contents page, which can be the plan, and the book prints as itself, since a page is a page.

It is the cheapest to build: the paginator exists, a page turn is a small interaction, and a world becomes the facing page and the borders. It is also where a world is weakest, because a world that lives in a page border is a skin, and every spread in the meadow has the same border. What the book loses is accumulation in space, the year at a distance and working in the margin across a lesson, and it has to cut a lesson's sections into fixed pages, which on a phone means a page too small to read or a page that scrolls, and a page that scrolls is a canvas with one axis.

### C. A roll: one sheet wide, running downward, divided into worlds

The journal is a roll of squared paper one sheet wide. A day is a row: its sheets sit in the single column, one under the other, and the world is drawn in the margins either side. Days run down the roll in the order they were done. A term is a stretch of the roll in one world, and each stretch begins with that world's horizon, drawn side on, so moving into a new term is coming over a horizon into a new place. In the terms of the question, it is a canvas bounded on one axis, with the bound across rather than along, and a world for each term down its length.

On Monday the roll opens at today, which is always the bottom of what has been written, with the date on a tag above the sheet and the guide standing on the path beside it. Yesterday is straight up, with the working the child left on it and in the margin beside it. A five year old finds their place because there is only one way to be lost, which is up, and one button that comes back. Zooming out turns the days into a strip of pages with their dates, which is flicking back through the book, and further out the roll hands over to the year's three worlds side by side. A parent sees the same roll with the answers on each sheet, a note beside each finished day, and the worlds they can change.

The cost is moderate and mostly already paid: the camera and the paper are the map's, the sheets are the lesson renderer's, the geometry is one column, and the world is drawn against a known width, which is what lets it be a place rather than a tile.

### The argument, and the recommendation

We recommend the third answer, the roll. The journal is one column of squared paper that runs downward through the year, with a row for each day and that day's sheets one under the other in it, so the past is always above, today is always at the bottom of what has been written, and nothing the child has done is further away than straight up. It keeps what made the infinite canvas worth trying, which is that a sheet stays where it was done with the working the child left beside it, and that zooming out turns the days into the term without turning a page. What it gives up is the second direction: a child cannot start a piece of work somewhere off to the side of the year, and the roll will never double as a map of the curriculum. We think that is the right thing to give up, because the child we are designing for is five, and a canvas a child can get lost on is a canvas they need an adult to find their place on.

The roll also wins on the part the owner cares about most. A world needs room to be a place, a known width to be composed against, and a moment of arrival. The infinite canvas gives room but no width, so a world there is texture that repeats until it runs out. The book gives width but no room, so a world there is a border. The roll gives both margins down its whole length, and a horizon at the top of each stretch that the child passes through.

What the infinite canvas gives up by losing is free placement: in the roll, a child's own drawing goes in the margin of the day it was made on, not anywhere they like. What the book gives up is the page turn as a gesture children already know, which the roll replaces with a Next button that moves the paper up to the next part of the sheet, and a one to one match between the screen and the printed pile. The sheet on the roll is the lesson's screen form, one continuous page that reflows on a phone; printing a day produces the same lesson through the paginator as numbered A4 or Letter pages. They are the same content from the same renderer, but they do not break in the same places, and the book would have made them identical.

## The roll, as built

A day is a row. Its sheets are 42 squares wide, the width of the printed page, so a scene that fits the print fits the roll, and on a phone they are 19 squares wide and the lesson reflows. Each sheet is placed on a whole square so its own grid lines up with the paper under the world. A day holds one or two sheets: a maths lesson starts a day and a lesson from another track that sits beside it joins it, which gives about two lessons a day for a family doing everything, as [tracks.md](tracks.md) says a year is. A day behind the child holds what was done on it; today holds what is planned for it. Which day is today comes from the plan's days (`nowIn` in `school/family/now.ts`), not from the maths path alone: today is the day of the last lesson the plan puts the child on, and it holds every lesson the plan has for today in the tracks that are on, so a lesson that shares no day with maths can still be today, and a family with maths off still has a today and a world the child stands in (17 September 2026).

The path runs down one margin beside a day's sheets, crosses under them to the date tag of the next day, and runs down the other margin beside that one, so it passes every day and never crosses paper. What comes next is a closed sheet under today with its title and nothing to open, because the journal holds what has been done and the map holds what is planned. When today's term is the last one reached, the next world's horizon is drawn below it, faded, with a sign on the path, and nothing further: the roll ends where the year is going next rather than trailing every term still to come.

The journal has its own three distances. Close in, at a zoom of 0.42 and above, the child reads and writes, and the pencil, highlighter, undo and the buttons for the next and last part of the sheet are on screen. A step out, the days are pages with their pictures and the date tags grow so they can be read, which is flicking back through the book. Below 0.11 each sheet becomes its title, and there the roll hands over to the place the world is seen as, which the next section describes; pulling back out of the place hands over to the map of every world, which opens on the year's land and steps out once more to all twelve. Each band has a small hysteresis, so a zoom that comes to rest on a threshold does not flicker. Escape steps out one distance at a time, from a sheet to the days, from the days to the place, and from the place to the map.

Working is kept with the day it was written on. Each stroke is stored against the day's id, measured from the top-left corner of the day's first sheet, and drawn in a layer that moves with the day. The map's ink keeps strokes in world coordinates, and in a journal that fails as soon as a sheet higher up grows by a line: every day below moves and its working stays behind. The rules for which pointer draws are the map's: a pen always draws, and the first pen switches the tool to the pencil; a finger draws only once the pencil is picked and no pen has been seen; a second finger hands the first to the camera for a pinch.

Only what is near the camera is painted. A world's washes are a few rectangles and go down at once; its horizon, each tile of its ground and path, and each drawing beside the path wait until the camera comes within a screen of them, and are then painted nearest first, a few milliseconds at a time between frames. The first version painted everything up front, and most of that time turned out to be asking the browser for points along the path, a few thousand times a stretch, to place planks and sleepers; the path is now kept as the curves it is made of and walked in plain arithmetic. Measured in the development server, the journal used to be ready in 1.6 seconds early in grade one and 2.9 to 4.1 seconds late in a year; it is now ready in 1.1 to 1.2 seconds in every grade at every point in the year, and 0.7 seconds of that is building the workspace, which parses and verifies the whole corpus and which a real build replaces with a compiled pack. Painting what is on screen takes about ten milliseconds. After the third pass, with twelve worlds, deep skies and the map, we measured it again the same way, twice for each of the four grades at each of the three points in the year. On a quiet machine it was ready in 0.84 to 1.04 seconds. Later the same day, with other work loading the machine, it was ready in 1.66 to 1.84 seconds, of which building the workspace took 1.49 to 1.56 seconds; the journal's own work after the workspace, which is building the sheets, laying out the roll and painting what is on screen, took 43 to 188 milliseconds, the most late in a year. So the journal's share has not grown, and the total moves with the workspace, which a compiled pack replaces. The map adds nothing to the time to open, because it is not built until it is first opened; building it then takes about ten milliseconds, and its pictures and roads are painted as the camera comes near them.

The journal holds more than maths, because it holds whatever the day held. At "just into term 2" in grade one it carries ten days and fifteen sheets across maths, physics, chemistry, coding and music, and five of those days carry two sheets. A game never prints, so it is not a sheet: it is a card beside the day that opens it, chosen as the game whose paper version is a question on that day's sheets or, failing that, one that works a skill those questions work. The bonds to ten and making ten days carry "Balance the pans", and the day with the music lesson carries a card that opens the keyboard. A played round does not yet leave a line on its card; that needs the `round-played` event read back, which is in the store and not wired here.

A page may lay its own things over the roll, and draw a world away from it. `World` takes an `over` slot, called once for each day the roll draws, with that day's view and the whole row's rectangle in roll units; what it returns is placed above the sheets in those same units, so it travels with the roll as the camera moves. The layer itself takes no pointer, so the paper behind it still drags, and what the page puts in it takes its own. `paintPicture` in `engine/ui/scenery.ts` draws a world as one small still picture of its horizon, 1500 by 980 in its own units for the page to scale, from the picture and the drawings a view already carries: the sky, the ground, a few marks of that ground, the drawings on the horizon and in the sky, and the gate. It holds no guide, since it stands for the place and not for the child in it, and nothing in it moves, so a page can put one beside every child's name without starting anything. Both were asked for by the grown-ups' app, which shows a child's place on the home and in the calendar's year, and both come with the world painter through `worldPainter()` in `engine/ui/painters.ts`, so neither is in what a page loads first (17 September 2026).

## A world as a place

Status: decided by the owner, 19 September 2026, from the three forms of the scratchpad's `world-map.html`. Zooming out of a world used to give a column of small sheets, which is what the page form already shows, so the canvas had no reason of its own at that distance. Zoomed out, a world is now a place: a trail through the world's own land with a stop for each day of the term, the guide standing at today, the landmarks lit by the lessons beside them, and the moment waiting at the trail's end. Zooming in on a stop, or tapping it, hands over to the roll for reading at that day, and pulling back out of the roll hands back to the place.

A stop's place comes from the day's number in the term and never from how tall its sheets are, so a sheet that grows moves nothing in the place, and the whole term is laid out from its first day. That is the journal's hardest constraint answered by construction rather than by care: the model is `school/worlds/trail.ts`, which is pure and tested, the shapes are in `engine/space.ts` beside the roll's, and `engine/ui/place.tsx` draws them with the roll's own painter, so the ground, the path, the horizon and everything beside the trail are the same code that paints a stretch of the roll.

What a stop says, a step out, is a postcard from the place. The question the day is about leads, in the world's own words, which is the line the drawing beside it already says; the lesson's name is small underneath; and the day is said as a child says it, Today, Yesterday, Last Thursday, Before the weekend, with the day's own stamped date past the week, since a whole term of days said in words reads as one phrase repeated. A finished day carries a stamp with the world's own creature in the ring, a gull at the harbour and a hen at the meadow, the way a passport is stamped where you went. The day still to come is a marker standing in the world rather than an empty card, and it does not name its lesson: a name a child cannot open yet is the plan's, and the plan is the map's.

A finished day draws the day's own sheet, as the child left it, into the box its stop was given, read-only and with the answers they wrote. The paper is asked for as it comes near the camera and let go of as it goes away, through the same door the roll asks through, so a whole term can be walked without the page growing; the box is fixed before the paper arrives and the postcard stands in it until it does, so nothing moves when it lands. The sheet stands in its box whole, shrunk to fit, and reading it properly is what going in is for.

Nothing is laid over a world any more. The buttons that went are Go in and the map's zoom pair, and the roll's The map, Today and its zoom pair, on the owner's instruction that moving in the canvas is enough. Tapping a world on the map, or pinching into it, goes in; pulling back out of the roll reaches the place and out of the place the map; Enter goes in, Escape comes out one step at a time, and the plus and minus keys zoom, which every canvas takes from the moment its screen opens. A screen reader reaches every stop as a named button. The one control over the roll and over the place is the way back in the top corner (`engine/ui/wayout.tsx`): out of sight until a hand moves over the paper or a finger lifts from it, there for a few seconds once the paper has stopped, and never on screen while the paper moves, so a child who has not found that pulling back is the way out still has one, and it is not in the way of a child pulling the camera about. It is low against the paper, soft ink on near-white, and a whole button, 48 px and 44 on a phone. The keyboard and a reader reach it on focus as before. Under reduced motion every handover is a cut, and the way back appears and goes without a fade.

## The journal as a page

The owner asked whether the journal could also be read as an ordinary page, scrolled from top to bottom the way the grown-up's journal reads, beside the infinite canvas rather than instead of it. The page form of the Worlds tab, `world.html?form=page`, is that page. It is the same roll: the days, the worlds, the reaches, the moments and the dates come from the same record and the same `layoutRoll`, and every drawing on it is painted by the same functions in `engine/ui/scenery.ts` that paint the canvas, with the same motion from `school/worlds/motion.ts` and the same stylesheet, `journal.css`. There is no second copy of any world, so a world that is added or redrawn appears in both forms at once.

### What the page holds

The page holds a term. It opens at today, and above today it runs up through the term's days to the arrival of the term's world; below today it ends with the next world's arrival, faded, when this term is the last one reached, which is where the canvas's roll ends too. The terms before this one are a button above the arrival, so a phone's page stays short and quick to open, and a link to an earlier day loads them before it scrolls there. A visit to a world, `world.html?place=marsh&form=page`, opens at that world's arrival, as the canvas does, and everything the address says (`place`, `grade`, `when`, `who`, `motion`) means the same in either form.

Each world is a stretch of the page. Its arrival is a window onto the world's horizon, drawn at whatever scale fits the whole horizon across the page, so the sky, the far row, the name, the gate and the guide's line are all in it at any width. Below the arrival each day is a section in the page's own flow: the day's date on its tag, and its sheets as real text that reflows, at the scale they are read at. Behind the days is one more window onto the rest of the stretch, at the same scale as the sheets, and in front of them another for what is laid on the paper: the tape at each sheet's corners, the guide on the path pointing at today's sheet with the creatures that follow them, a game's card, a grown-up's note and the child's working, which the page shows and the canvas writes. Because the sections are in the flow, the browser scrolls, keeps its place and prints them; because the windows are drawn from the same layout at the same scale as the sections, the path, the tape and every drawing line up with the sheets as they do on the canvas.

The scale of a day is the sheet with the world either side of it, as the grown-up's journal shows one child, and never smaller than the canvas reads a sheet at: 0.74 at 1440 pixels, 0.62 on a small laptop, and on a phone the narrow sheet nearly the width of the screen. Where an arrival meets the days the two scales differ, a little on a laptop and a lot on a phone, so the arrival fades out as the world behind the days fades in, rather than the path jumping sideways. On a phone the arrival is a picture about a third of the size the days are read at, and the drawings that stand beside the sheets are out of view, as they are on the canvas at reading distance; a card a drawing says, such as the market stall's line beside the coins lesson, is left off where there is no room for it beside the sheet, rather than cut in half at the edge of the screen.

### Motion and settling

The page keeps the canvas's rules for motion, because it calls the same code. It is a stage in `journal.css`'s sense, so the one amplitude that eases every movement to rest applies to it. The world settles when a sheet is under the middle of the screen and covers a quarter of it, which is `isWorking` in `motion.ts`, the canvas's own test, and wakes when the page is scrolled to an arrival, a date or the space between days; once it has eased to rest, every movement is paused. Anything off the screen is paused by an observer, as on the canvas, and a hidden tab pauses the rest. A tap on a drawing that answers is found with a hit area of at least 44 pixels, and the day's events play on the day they happened: a world's moment, a landmark lit by its lesson, a creature walking in behind the guide. Under reduced motion the world is still and complete. Two things do not come across. The rare sight that crosses a horizon after two minutes of resting on it is not played, because a page is rarely left resting on a horizon, and the secrets at the edges of each world stay on the canvas, because they are found by wandering sideways, which a page cannot do.

### Printing

Printed, each world's arrival is a picture across the top of its first page, without the guide's line, which is said on the screen, and each day is its date and its sheets at the printed width, breaking between a lesson's questions and paragraphs rather than through one. The world behind and beside the days is left off the paper, since a margin of scenery costs ink and adds nothing a child works on. The page works out its printed layout whenever it lays itself out, so it prints the same whether or not the browser warns it first, and it paints every arrival as it is built, since an arrival is the one part of the world that is printed. This is the journal as it was, which is closer to a keepsake than to work; the pile a child works on is still what the lessons page prints. The term the page opens on in grade one "just into term 2", three days of the harbour, prints to 14 A4 pages. One thing prints wrongly: in Chrome's PDF the harbour's sand is drawn to one side of its water, where the screen draws it in the middle, and we have not found why. We have not tried printing each lesson's paginated form in place of its screen form, which would be shorter and would answer the open question below about a cover sheet for each world.

### Moving between the canvas and the page

The choice is the Canvas | Page switch in the Worlds tab's row, and the page has a bar over it with Today, The map, which opens the canvas at the map, and Zoom and draw, which opens the canvas. Choosing a form remembers it on this device under `lumischool.journal.form.v1`, and a year or a world opened without `form` in its address opens in the form remembered. With nothing remembered, a phone (640 pixels or narrower, the canvas's own phone width) opens the page and anything wider opens the canvas. A link that names a form with `form=`, or asks for something only the canvas has (a place on it, a place on the map, an arrival), is followed as it is. The whole map, the worlds to come and the scenes further off open as canvases unless the link says otherwise, since being seen whole is what they are for.

The choice belongs to the device rather than to the child, because it follows the screen. We made the page the default on a phone because a phone is where native scrolling, text that reflows and a back button that keeps your place matter most, and where a canvas two directions wide is hardest to find your place on; the canvas stays the default on a tablet and a laptop because that is where the pencil, the zoom and the map are, and they are the reasons the canvas exists. We have not put either default in front of a child.

### What it costs, and what it shares

Measured when the two forms were still pages of their own and alternated four times each in the development server with other work loading the machine, the page was drawn 1.27 to 1.75 seconds after it was opened and the canvas 1.59 to 2.75 seconds. Building the workspace took 1.1 to 2.4 seconds of each; the page's own work after it, building the term's sheets, laying out the roll and painting what is on screen, took 130 to 215 milliseconds, against 250 to 410 for the canvas's. The page is quicker because it holds one term and builds only that term's sheets. The check that sends a device to its remembered form runs before the workspace is built, so it adds nothing we can measure to the canvas. We have not measured the two forms on a quiet machine.

The page used to repeat about two hundred lines of `journal.ts` that were page code rather than world code: reading the year, the record and a visit from the link, finding a day's reach and the game beside it, how the record leaves each drawing beside the path, and the controls above. When the two forms joined the Worlds tab that part moved into `school/worlds/view.ts`, which reads nothing from the page, and both forms read it there. What is left in each is its own: the canvas's camera, sheets and map in `src/pages/world-canvas.ts`, and the page's windows, sections and print in `src/pages/world-page.ts`.

We considered and did not build three other shapes. One scale for the arrival and the days keeps the world continuous, and on a phone it crops the horizon to its middle and loses the gate, which is what the grown-up's journal does at that width. A mode of the canvas's module would share its code directly, and it would put a second page into a file of 1,800 lines that other work was changing, where a form of its own shares the world through `src/world` and leaves the canvas alone. And sheets placed absolutely over one scaled world, as the grown-up's roll places them, are simpler, and a printer would cut them through the middle of a question.

## The Worlds tab

The owner found five tabs that each showed some view of the worlds, and asked for one flexible tab that shows any of them with little in the way of the map. Journey was the year as a plan. Journal was the year as a roll with the map of every world at the top of its zoom, drawn as a canvas; Journal page was the same roll as a page; Map poster was the map printed; and Worlds to come was the proposed worlds drawn on the fourth year's map with a card for each. The last four drew the same worlds with the same code in `school/worlds/` and `src/world/` and differed only in where they stood, how the view was read and what a grown-up saw, so they are now one page, `world.html`, labelled Worlds. Journey stays a tab of its own, for the reason the section above on one canvas at two distances gives: it is the plan, drawn as lessons with their locks and prerequisites, where a map of worlds shows places and what was done in them and has no locks at all. It draws no world, and its code goes to the grown-ups' app rather than to `school/worlds/` ([structure.md](structure.md)).

The tab has one slim row above the view, and the view takes the rest of the screen. The drawings in a roll's margin stand beside the sheet rather than out in the middle of the margin (20 September 2026): each is 70 units clear of the sheet's edge, and no wider than 230, so at reading distance on a 1440 window, which shows 720 units either side of the roll's middle, the whole of a drawing and the line under it are in view. The line takes the same 230 and wraps. The cap has a floor: a drawing is never drawn at fewer than 12 pixels for one of its own squares, which is what a landmark beside a sheet needs to be recognised, measured on the drawings themselves rather than argued. The other half of that rule is the one that bites: a drawing carrying words, or parts meant to be counted, needs question size, about 20 pixels a square, so it belongs on the sheet or in a narrower take rather than in a margin. That is fineness rather than width. Six drawings are under the floor today, the train, the ramp, the departures board, the fossil, the planets and the magnet, and they stand capped until narrower takes of them are drawn. At 1280 a wide drawing loses up to 80 units and at 1024 the sheet's own 840 leaves no margin at reading distance at all, which no anchor can mend.

The guide keeps its own place rather than the landmarks' (the owner, 20 September 2026). A landmark is anchored 70 units from the sheet's edge, while the guide stands where it always has, its near edge 154 units out and its far edge at 324. The reason is that the guide is not a 170-unit drawing but a group about 500 across: it carries the creatures that have joined the child, which walk up the trail to it as a day plays, and the trail itself runs 110 units from the sheet, so a guide at a landmark's distance would stand on the way rather than beside it and bring its followers onto it. The cost of leaving it is that a narrow window loses it: at 1440 a child reading sees all but the outer 24 units of it, at 1280 about a third of it, and at 1024 none of it at all, which the owner has seen and accepted for now. The two are rendered side by side in .scratchpad/leftover/map-move/guide/guide-now.png and guide-in.png. Moving the guide in honestly means moving the lane with it, which is a change to how a roll is laid out rather than a number, and it has not been done.

A past sheet shows the answers of the lesson's last screen sitting only when that sitting was made from the lesson as it is now, which the sitting's lesson hash says. A lesson done on paper says its answers are on the sheet a grown-up marked, and one that has changed since says the answers given then are not shown, since the questions drawn now may not be the ones that were answered. The roll asks for the past sheets near where the camera rests, all of that rest's at once, and lays itself out again round them only when the camera is still, keeping the day under the camera where it was, so nothing is swapped under a moving finger or wheel. The camera follows a sheet only when the child starts working in a question: typing, a tap, or a key that does something there. A press that may be the start of dragging the paper, the keys that move about the page or along a box, and anything within a second of the viewer moving the paper themselves leave the camera where it is, so a grown-up can look back up the roll without being brought back to today's question. The zoom a rule reads is written where it is read rather than on the roll. A custom property set on the world is inherited by everything on it, so writing the zoom there on every frame of a pinch restyled the whole roll, about 6,000 elements a frame, which is most of what a pinch cost (measured 277 ms of style over 270,000 elements while pinching out and 408 ms over 504,000 pinching in, against 33 ms and 28 ms with those writes taken out; the level change and the rescale of what idles made no measurable difference). So `--grow` is written on the flags layer, whose dates, names and signs grow as the child draws back, `--iz` on the covers while the roll is far off, and `--iz` on the world when the camera comes to rest, which is soon enough for the motion and the touch targets sized by it. Going in waits for today's sheets while the map dives into the place. A lazily loaded part of the child's page that cannot be fetched, as when a tab outlives the code it was opened with, loads the page again once, and if the world's code still cannot be had the page says "This is taking a long time." after fifteen seconds and offers Try again, rather than leaving "Your page is opening." up for ever.

## The map of every world

Zooming out past the year, the roll hands over to a map of every world of every year, each world a place standing in a country of lands and seas, joined to the next by the way the story travels there: a path, a road, rails, a river, and a crossing by water to the island. It replaces the year's shelf of worlds that the first two passes had at the same distance. The country is set out in [overworld.md](overworld.md): four lands in one sea, one for each year, lying clockwise round the sea from the south-west, and a year ends by sailing to the next land. The fourth year climbs the mountains of its own land, comes down their river to the open sea and crosses to the island in the water between the lands. A child's map is the land of the year they stand in, with the sea round it, and a grown-up's map shows all four lands; finishing a year is the sail to the next land.

We considered three places for it. The brief suggested it be the top of the journal's zoom, world, then year, then everything, replacing the shelf; that is what we built, because it is the step the roll's own far view was already reaching for, and because a child who zooms out of their book should find the rest of their book rather than a different product. The second was to fold it into the year map on `journey.html`, which already has a shelf of four years. We did not, for the reason the two surfaces were kept apart in the first place: the year map is the plan, drawn as lessons with their locks and prerequisites, and the map of worlds is places and what was done in them, with no locks at all. They share the camera, the paper and the year model, and a grown-up still plans on the year map. The third was a separate page, which would have lost the zoom that joins the child's day to the whole school.

The map is geometry in `school/worlds/overworld.ts` over the country in `school/worlds/geography.ts`, pure like the roll's, with each term's place and the ways between them keyed by the term's slot (`1.2` for year 1, term 2), so whichever world a family puts in a term stands in that term's place and travels its roads, and a world made to be chosen stands beside it; it is and is drawn by `engine/ui/map.ts` as an illustrated map, each world composed in its own declaration as a few of its own shelf drawings standing on the land ([overworld.md](overworld.md)). A child's map draws the land of their year edge to edge: every world and place of the year, dimmed until its way opens, and nothing of another year's land; it has no year labels, opens on the whole land, draws back no further than it, and on a phone opens closer, on the place the child stands at. A grown-up's map, in the "As a grown-up" view, draws the whole country with the land ahead in pencil, the years lettered on it and the "Every year" zoom. A way not yet taken is a pencil line of the same shape, and it is inked in its own kind once the child has done work beyond it. The map has its own camera on its own layer over the roll, which stays where it was underneath, and it is built the first time it is opened.

### The ground between the worlds

The worlds stand in a country laid out in `school/worlds/geography.ts` and made into coasts, rivers, lakes and the ground round each world in `school/worlds/terrain.ts`, drawn in the same hand and the same five markers as the worlds. The land is green country with the ground of each world washed round its picture: fields by the meadow, sand at the harbour, heather on the night hill, snow round the mountains. The sea is always washed, and every coast is drawn with water lines following it out and shallows along it. Each land has a river: the first year's runs from its fells past the meadow to the sea, the second's from the forest lake past the kitchen to the town's estuary, the third's out of the sea loch, and the fourth's down from the mountains to the firth the open sea lies off, which is the way from the mountains to the open sea. Where a way over land meets water it goes over on a bridge, and a way from one year's land to the next is a sea lane. The kitchen is a cottage on the map and the laboratory a hall, each with its world's picture for a window. The windmill, the bridge, the cottage and the jetty were drawn for the first version and added to the shelf's "Places and creatures" in the files they belong in.

The ground follows the rule the worlds and the roads follow: it is drawn twice from the same shapes, once in pencil and once washed and inked, which shows only inside the child's reach. On a child's map the pencil is drawn over the whole of their land. The reach is worked out each time from the same journey the rewards are, so nothing new is stored. It is a set of soft circles: round every world with work in it, along every way the child has gone, and round the world the child is in at a size that grows with the share of its lessons finished and creeping a little along the way ahead; a land whose worlds are all finished is coloured to its coasts, with the islands off it. So the colour spreads across the map as the year goes on and the pencil edge is always just ahead of the child. The island stays in pencil until the child reaches it, whatever is washed near it. When the map opens, the colour washes out across the land the last lesson reached, on the engine's spring; nothing records what the map looked like last time, so it is the last lesson's reach each time.

The ground is kept quieter than the worlds. Its washes are shares of the same cap as a world's, lighter than the pictures' own, so the pictures, their stamps and their names stay the loudest things on the map, and every name, stamp and year label sits on a patch of paper. The test composites the washes under every name and over the water, the island and each world's ground, and holds ink on each to 7 to 1 as if the patch were not there. Only the washes, the coast, the rivers and the lakes are painted when the map is built, which added about five milliseconds to the ten it took; the marks that make the ground a landscape (waves, furrows, drifts, clumps of trees) and the drawings standing in it are painted a tile at a time when the camera is near, and the marks wait until the camera is close enough to see them, so the whole map at its widest is washes and a coast. The sea moves very slightly and the island's smoke rises; under reduced motion both are still. On the Worlds tab only the tiles of sea on the screen drift, they rest where the camera is so far out that the drift would be under a pixel, and they stand still with `motion=off` or once a family has stopped a world's creatures moving; while the map is open, nothing on the roll under it moves.

The ground also gives the journey words. Each way has a phrase worked out from what it passes, for walking it either way: "down to the coast", "along the coast", "over the bridge", "up the path to the door", "down the river to the sea", "across the sea" from one year's land to the next, "across the water to the island". The live region says it on arrival ("Across the sea to the woods.") and the list of worlds after the journal says it for each world. The tests check that every world stands on land but the open sea, that the island is an island of its own to the north of the harbour and on the harbour's horizon as its glimpse, that the harbour has the sea at its back, that no way over land runs through the sea off its bridges, that the way between two years is by sea, and that the crossing to the island is over the water.

The keyboard travels. On the map the arrow keys follow the roads from world to world: from each world, the arrow that points along a road to the world before or after it on the run goes there, riding what that way is (a train on rails, a bus on a road, a narrowboat down the river, a ship across the sea, the hot-air balloon up through the clouds, on foot along a path), and an arrow that points where no road goes says so aloud, naming the ways that do go, rather than moving the map. On a child's map a world the child's learning has not opened the way to yet says so and stays where it is. P flies the paper plane ([overworld.md](overworld.md)), F shows the map full screen, Enter goes into the world, Escape steps out to every year on a grown-up's map and back to the child's own part on a child's, T goes back to today, Home and End go to the first world and the furthest the child may go, and shift with an arrow pans. Each world is a button with a roving tab stop, whose name says the world, the year and term, whether it is finished, here, begun or not reached, the day it was stamped and its moment if it has happened. A live region says each arrival. After the journal the map is also a list, which is what a screen reader reads without the canvas. A grown-up's list holds all twelve worlds in the order the roads join them, each with its year and term, what has happened there and a button to go in. A child's list holds only what the child's map shows, the worlds reached and the next one, in the words the map's own buttons say, with no year numbers and no counts, and the island's smoke far off.

The transitions use the engine's own primitives from `src/engine/` rather than a second set: its closed-form springs, its timelines with cues, and its ticker. Opening the map arrives from the child's own world, with the camera starting close on its picture and drawing back on a critically damped spring to the child's own part of the country, or to the year on a grown-up's map. Travelling from one world to the next is a timeline with one track and two cues: the guide walks the road and the camera goes with it, lifting a little in the middle so both worlds are in view, and the arrival is announced on the last cue. Going into a world is one movement carried across two views, since in the child's app the map and the roll are separate components that are never on the page at once (17 September 2026). The map dives into the world's picture, holding the place still while the country falls away behind it, and hands the page the box that picture ended in; whatever the page draws for that world, the roll or a layout of its own, opens with its horizon in the same box and grows out of it, so there is no cut between the two. Coming back out is that movement reversed: the roll pulls back and fades into a box in the middle of the screen, the map opens with the place drawn there, and the country pulls back round it. The box travels as a plain rectangle in the page's own pixels (`onGoIn` and `arrive` on `Overworld`, `from` and `onLeave` on `World`), and `cameraOn` in `engine/space.ts` is the one piece of arithmetic behind it, the camera that puts a box of the world on a box of the screen, so neither view needs to know what the other draws. The zoom is stepped geometrically rather than linearly (`cameraBetween`), so a dive across a long distance reads at one speed instead of racing at the far end. On the Worlds tab it was one timeline with four cues, the camera diving into the picture while the roll was put at the world's horizon underneath; the world assembling itself (what stands on its horizon, its name, its gate and the guide rising into place one after another, each on a spring with a small overshoot) is still the roll's own arrival, and when it is the child's own world the camera goes on down through the year to today's sheet. Another year's world opens that year's journal at the same place, where it assembles in the same way. Under reduced motion every timeline is collapsed before it runs, which the engine provides: every track is at its end from the start and every cue fires at once, in order, so every flight is a cut, the world is already assembled, and the child lands in exactly the place the animation would have taken them. Going in and coming back out cut in the same way: no box is handed over, and each view opens where it stands. The paper plane is a game on the engine's fixed loop, reading the same `Pad` the Games tab's action games read, in `engine/motion/plane.ts` and `src/world/fly.ts`; under reduced motion its loop does not run, and each press flies it a little way, drawn once where it got to.

What the child's work makes happen on the map, and on the roll, is set out in [story.md](story.md): a road inked by the first lesson beyond it, a stamp in each world by its first lesson, a landmark lit beside the day of the lesson it belongs to, a creature walking behind the guide after its lesson, the world's moment inked by the term's last lesson, and a secret at the edge of each world beside its third day. A term's last lesson is the last in the tracks the family's plan has on, read as the plan stood on each day, so a family doing only maths has its moments and turning a track on later takes none away. Every one of them is worked out from the finished lessons and their days each time the map is drawn, in `school/worlds/rewards.ts`, and nothing is stored beside the record. A grown-up sees the same map, and in the grown-up view each world carries a note with the lesson and the day behind each mark.

## Worlds

A world is the place a stretch of the year happens in. It themes everything around the paper and nothing on it.

### What a world is made of, as data

A world is a small declaration in `school/worlds/`, one file per world, that names its drawings by id rather than holding them. The table says where each part comes from today.

The marks a world draws itself (grass, ripples, planks, sleepers, clouds, gulls) live in `paint.ts` and not on the shelf, because they are texture rather than subjects: nobody would put one in a question, they have no anchors or parameters a lesson could vary, and a world needs hundreds of them.

What still needs new art is listed in each world's `wants`, and it is the honest limit of the model. The first three worlds asked for a rabbit, a gull, a crab, a lighthouse, a station and a fox, and those are drawn now; what they still want is a stile and a hedge for the meadow, a quay wall and a seal for the harbour, and a signal, a level crossing and a tunnel for the railway. Each of the later worlds lists two things of its own, from a badger for the woods and safety goggles for the laboratory to a mountain goat, a ship's wheel and a sea turtle for the fourth year.

### How many worlds a year needs, and what changes between them

A year has three worlds, one a term. A term is the stretch a family plans in and the stretch a child can remember as a place ("when we were at the harbour"), and three is few enough that each arrival is an event rather than a palette swap every fortnight. Four grades at three a year is twelve worlds, and all twelve are built. The fourth year used to borrow three of the first nine; it now has its own, which are older, bigger and further from home than anything before them.

What makes the move feel like arriving somewhere is that everything changes at once and it changes at a threshold the child passes. The ground changes kind, from grass to sand with water either side to an embankment. The path changes from a worn track to planks to rails. The light changes from green to orange and blue to pink. A different guide lives there: the firefly in the meadow, the paper bird at the harbour, the glow on the railway. The horizon is a different picture, the world's name is written across its sky, and the guide at the gate says where you are. The old world's ground fades out into the paper above the new world's sky, with a sign on the path that says where it goes, so leaving the meadow is walking out of it rather than turning a page.

Inside a world, the seasons turn along the stretch where a world has a drawing that turns: the tree beside the meadow's path is in summer early in the term and in autumn later, and the one beside the woods' path goes from autumn into winter. Only those two do this; the other worlds declare seasons that nothing on the shelf draws yet, though the firs can carry snow and a world could turn them.

### The twelve worlds, and why this set

We built whole years rather than one world a grade, because the two things a world has to prove are the arrival between one term and the next and the way its landmarks belong to that term's lessons, and both are only visible inside a year. A track place with lessons has a roll of its own, one term long, holding its lessons in grade order, and its stamp, its lit landmarks, its followers and its moment are worked out by rewards.ts from the same record as a term's.

Two smaller changes came with them. Since then every world plays its drawings' declared motion, and `FULL_MOTION` is gone: `engine/motion/world.ts` is what a world plays, with nothing of the page in it, and `engine/ui/player.ts` plays it. The twelve of the run each have a rare sight of their own and the wants their files listed, drawn and placed. The six concept scenes of [worlds-next.md](worlds-next.md) are built as places a track's lessons bring a child to, so `WORLDS` holds twenty-nine, and no two of them share a ground, a path, a light, a gate, a moment or a rare sight.

For each day, the first reach that one of the day's lessons holds puts that drawing beside the top of that lesson's sheet, a little larger than the rest, with its line written on a card under it in the world's hand, and the rest of the margin is laid out below it without drawing the same thing twice. The clock tower stands beside quarter past and quarter to and says "Read the clock on the tower."; the pizza stands beside halves, quarters and thirds with "Cut the pizza into equal parts."; the thermometer stands beside the water cycle with "Warm water evaporates faster." (the circuit lesson stands in the term before the laboratory's since the grade 3 lessons were re-ordered, so the circuit waits on the bench for the moment and reaches into no day).

Counted over each whole year in its own three worlds, every lesson of the run now has a landmark that belongs to it: all 60 of grade one's, 65 of grade two's, 63 of grade three's and 59 of grade four's, and so do the terms a family may choose the winter fair or the farm for, and the places off the run. A reach may name any drawing a world offers, not only those on its horizon, so the kitchen puts its oven beside a coding lesson and the town its umbrellas beside the lesson on changes you can undo. The test holds less than every lesson, so a new lesson does not break it, and [overworld.md](overworld.md) gives its target and why. It still checks that every drawing, skill and track a reach names is one the corpus actually has, so a reach cannot be written against a lesson that does not exist. What a reach says follows the arrival line's rule: at most eight words, because a child reads it.

### The paper is not themed, and the boundary is the design

A question sits on white squared paper in full colour, because that is what prints and what every drawing was tuned against. So the journal has two materials and the join between them is deliberate. The world is drawn on the roll itself, in marker washes and pencil over the squared paper, and fades out into plain paper at the sides and between stretches. A sheet is a piece of white squared paper laid on top of it, lined up with the same grid, with a hairline edge, a shadow under it, and two pieces of the world's tape at its top corners. The world's colour touches the sheet only there, inside the sheet's own top padding, and nothing a world places comes within three squares of a sheet. Read that way, the white rectangle in the middle of every world is the thing the child brought with them, and the world is where they sat down to do it.

Two smaller rules keep it looking deliberate rather than accidental. White belongs to the paper and to labels: a path is a tint of the world's markers, never white, so the eye goes to the sheet first. And every piece of writing a world adds (the dates, the signs, the guide's greeting) sits on a white card; the one exception is the world's name, which is written on the sky, inside the contrast rule below, and on a deep sky in that sky's own light ink.

### What stops a world making a question harder to read

No world can reach a question: the sheet is drawn with the paper tokens whatever world it sits in, and a world has no field that could change them. What a world can still do wrong is around the sheet, and each of those is a rule in `check.ts` that every world, and every world as a family could change it, has to pass before it is drawn.

A world may only use the five markers, so it cannot bring a colour the palette was never tuned against. A wash is capped at an opacity of 0.3 over paper, and ink on any marker at that cap keeps a contrast of at least 7 to 1, which the test computes from the paper colours in `base.css` and checks have not drifted. A world holds at most six landmarks and four creatures, so the margins stay quiet. The arrival line is at most eight words, because a five year old reads it. Scenery keeps three squares clear of every sheet at both widths, which the test checks against the layout rather than by eye. Creatures move only by small idle motions, only when the family leaves motion on, and never when the device asks for reduced motion, whatever the family set.

A family's change goes through the same rules. A pick the world does not offer is left out with a sentence saying so, and a change that would break a rule leaves the world as it comes and says why.

### The sky: how a night is dark and a dusk glows

The wash cap exists to keep paper white and anything written near it legible, and it was applied to the whole of a world, which is why the night sky used to be a pale blue wash with stars on it. The sky is the one part of a world that sits above both: no sheet comes within three squares of it, and the only thing written on it is the world's name. So the rule is now split in two.

Above the horizon a world may take a deep sky instead of a wash, from a short list declared once in `check.ts`: a night that runs from a dark navy at the top to a lighter blue at the horizon, a dusk from purple through pink to gold, a storm from slate to grey, and an ember glow from a dark plum to orange for the volcano island. A world cannot bring a dark colour of its own, only name one of the four. The deep sky stops at the horizon line, and everything below it, the ground and every sheet on it, keeps the existing cap, so the sheet stays white squared paper under a dark sky. It is laid a little short of solid, at 0.92, so the squares of the paper still show through it.

Anything written on a deep sky has its own ink or its own patch. Each deep sky carries a light ink for the world's name and a softer one for the term above it, and the test computes the contrast of each against every colour of the gradient over the band where a name can sit, over white paper at the sky's opacity, and holds it to 7 to 1, the same enhanced level as ink on a wash. The name is held to that band in the layout, and to the part of the sky's width that has not begun to fade into paper, which is why a long name grows less than a short one when the child steps back. It is also held inside the world's own width, which is all a phone shows at a world's arrival, so on the narrow roll a long name such as the volcano island's is written a little smaller rather than past the edge of its sky. A world not reached yet is drawn faded, which leaves its deep sky neither dark nor light, so there the name is written on a patch of paper. The date tags, what a landmark says, the line under a world's moment and the guide's arrival line are all below the horizon, and the test checks, for every deep-sky world in every term at both widths, that each of them and every sheet stays at least three squares from the deep sky. Drawings standing against a deep sky are given a light edge, since pencil on navy would vanish, and clouds and rain up there are drawn in the sky's light ink. A moon that would sit behind the name waits below it, because light ink over a yellow moon is the one place the name could not be read.

The night sky takes the night, the woods take the dusk, the open sea the storm and the volcano island the ember glow.

## Customisation

A parent may change which world each term is in, for each year, and so the order the child travels through them; the guide who lives in each world, from the designs that world offers; the colour of the ground, from the markers it offers; the weather; what stands beside the path and who lives there, within the counts above; whether the creatures move; and the name written on the journal, which is the child's display name that the product already stores.

A parent may not change the paper, the questions, the answers or what the guide says; the size of the writing or how much there is to read; the order of the lessons, which is the plan and is changed from the plan; anything that opens, locks or rewards a lesson, since a world is scenery and nothing is earned by reaching one; or the palette, including a dark ground. The panel shows both lists beside the choices, because a rule nobody can read is not a commitment, which is the principle `src/family/settings.ts` already follows.

The panel is read off the worlds rather than written for them: it lists every world of the run for every term, and for the world being changed it shows that world's own guides, grounds, weather, landmarks and creatures, so a tenth world appears in it by being added to the list. The preview is the journal itself. Each change is applied to the roll behind the panel as it is made, and the camera moves to where that world can be seen with a sheet in it: today's sheet if the child is in that world, or the world's horizon if they have not reached it. Nothing is kept until the parent presses Keep this, and Put it back returns to what was kept. A world picked for two terms in a row is allowed, and the panel says that there will be no arrival between them. A term with work in it keeps its world: the marks a child's work makes are drawn in the world the work was done in, so changing that world afterwards would take a lit landmark or a moment away, and the panel says why the choice is fixed.

The worlds made to be chosen widen the first of those choices without adding a kind. In the panel, each term's list holds the twelve, as it always has, and for a term a world is made for, an "Or, for this term" group holding it: the winter fair for the winter term of years 1 to 3, and the farm for the first term of year 1 and the last of year 2. A track place and a world of another year are never in a term's list, and `readChoice` keeps a chosen world only in a term it was made for, saying so otherwise. The rules above hold unchanged: a term with work in it keeps its world, and a family that changed nothing stores nothing.

A grown-up can also visit any world, whatever the child's year has reached, from the picker in the Worlds tab's row or at `world.html?place=` with a world's id or a word of its name (`?place=marsh`). A visit puts the world where it stands for that visit only and keeps nothing, and its working is kept under its own key, so the child's own journal and choice are unchanged by it. `&arrive=0` opens a visit at today rather than at the world's horizon, or for a world with no lessons yet at the card where its first sheet would be, and `&when=1` shows any year finished, with its moments inked. Without `when` a visit to a world of a term opens partway into that term, so the world is there as reached rather than faded.

What is stored is only what differs from each world's own choices, so a family that changed nothing stores nothing. It names worlds and drawings by id and holds no colour of its own and no position, so it cannot break a layout, and a world whose art improves after a family chose it improves for them. With a changed guide, ground and set of creatures it is under two hundred bytes, which the test holds it to. It is read back without trusting it: a world that no longer exists, a drawing that was renamed, a colour outside the palette or an unknown weather each fall back to the world's own choice with a sentence the parent sees, so a stale choice always opens to a journal rather than to a blank ground. The prototype keeps it in the browser under `lumischool.journal.worlds.<child>.v1`.

## What the theming model needs from the data model

A world is content and a choice is a setting, and the two go in different places.

Worlds ship in the pack like lessons do. They are small declarations with stable ids, and a child's device resolves them offline from the pack, which is what `readChoice` and `apply` already assume. A world is not evidence and should not be recorded on a sitting: which world a sheet was done in says nothing about the child's maths.

What the child's work makes happen on the map needs nothing new: it is worked out from the finished lessons and their days, which the fold of `sitting-ended` and `marked` already produces. We considered and rejected an event for a secret found, for the reasons in [story.md](story.md).

The choice needs a per-child setting that the parent's device writes and the child's device only reads. Under the recommended local-first log and the store as built, there are two ways to hold it: a twelfth event kind beside `plan-changed`, folded to the current choice, or a column on `children`. We would take the event. The log is the store's one write path, a parent's device may be offline when they change a world, the history of changes comes for free, and the rule for two devices changing the same choice is the one [data-model.md](data-model.md) already asks plan events to state: the last writer wins and the other device shows a notice that its change was overwritten. The payload is the trimmed choice above, which holds a year of terms only for the grades a family changed. This needs the owner's decision, because it adds an event kind to `engine/answer.ts`, which the store declares once.

A world is chosen per term, and a term is a position in the plan, not a date. Today a term is derived as three units in a row, which is how the map draws the year; if the plan shifts a unit into the next term, the world moves with it. The plan needs to say where its terms begin, and [tracks.md](tracks.md) makes this sharper, since a track's lessons are placed by the plan rather than by week numbers.

The child's working is the open part. It is the child's own book, and the prototype keeps it on the device per day. The list in `src/family/privacy.ts` of what leaves the child's build says attempt rows and nothing else, so working in the margin would stay on the device unless it becomes part of an attempt, which is the drawing answer kind [data-model.md](data-model.md) specifies and has not measured. If it stays on the device, a child who changes tablet loses their margins; if it leaves, it is the largest thing a child's device sends. We have not measured how large a day's working is.

## Accessibility and restraint

The journal is light only, like the rest of the product. Every control a child taps over the paper is 48 pixels, and 44 on a phone, where the tab's row sits above the canvas rather than on the paper and zooming is a pinch. Camera flights are instant when the device asks for reduced motion, which the map's view already does, and the creatures and guides stay still whatever the family set. The keyboard moves along the roll with the arrows, zooms with plus and minus, goes to today with T, steps through the parts of a sheet with N and P, steps out with Escape, opens the map with M and shows it full screen with F; on the map the arrows travel along the roads, as described above. The sheets are real text rather than pictures of text, a live region says where the journal has gone when a button or a key moves it, the whole roll is also a list of days after it, grouped by world, with a link to each lesson on its own, and the map is a list of worlds after that. The deep skies are scenery and not a theme: the page around the journal, every control and every sheet stay light. Under reduced motion the camera's flights and the map's transitions are cuts, the worlds do not assemble, and a found secret does not move. The worlds' own small movements, and the rule that they settle while a sheet has the child's attention, are in [motion.md](motion.md), built so far in the harbour.

Two things are not done on the canvas. A screen reader cannot yet step from day to day inside the roll the way the map lets Tab step from lesson to lesson, so the list is the reliable route; the journal as a page is the other one, since each of its days is a section headed by its date and its sheets are in the order they are read. And the world's own labels are drawn at a size that is legible on screen but not tested against the reading load rules the curriculum sets for questions, because a world's labels are dates and names rather than instructions.

## Open questions

- Whether the year map on `journey.html` stays a separate surface. The owner decided it does not, and the page was deleted on 17 September 2026, since the child's map is places and what was done in them and nothing else read the year map.
- Where the child's working lives: on the device as the child's own book, or as evidence with the attempt it sits beside. This is a privacy decision as much as a data one.
- Whether the roll should hold the lesson's screen form, as built, or its printed pages. The printed pages would make the screen and the pile identical, at the cost of a sheet too wide to read on a phone without panning sideways.
- Whether a world is chosen per term, as built, or could be per track, so that coding always happens on the railway. Per track would make a day with two sheets sit in two worlds, which the roll cannot draw.
- The wants lists for the twelve, and whether a world's art is shelf art (usable in questions) or world art (not).
- Whether a landmark that reaches into a lesson should take the lesson's own numbers: the clock tower showing the time of the lesson's first clock, the grandstand holding the rows the lesson asks about. The drawings already take numbers, so it is a small step, and it would make a reach part of the lesson rather than a picture beside it.
- Whether arriving in a new world for the first time should play the arrival from the map on its own, rather than when the child goes in from the map. The arrival is built; when it plays is not decided.
- Whether a child should choose anything themselves. The product has the parent customise; one small choice, a creature, might be the child's.
- Whether a printed term should carry a cover sheet with its world's horizon on it, as a divider in the pile, since that is the one place a world could reach paper without touching a question. The journal as a page already prints each world's arrival above its first day, in the screen form; printing each lesson's paginated form under that picture is not tried.
- Whether the page should hold one term, as built, or the whole year as the canvas does. One term keeps a phone's page short and is where a child is; the year is one button away.
